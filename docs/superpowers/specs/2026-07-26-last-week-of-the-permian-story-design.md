# The Last Week of the Permian Story Design

Date: 2026-07-26
Status: approved written design; detailed architecture awaits Phase 2C and Permian research

## Purpose

Define the narrative architecture for *The Last Week of the Permian*, the
first expedition in the *Time Expedition* anthology.

The story must read as a credible unpublished H. G. Wells manuscript from the
late 1890s. It continues the story-world of *The Time Machine* while remaining
shorter than that novella. Its central movement is from scientific detection
to environmental survival: the Time Traveller investigates a sequence of
ecological irregularities until the evidence itself becomes an immediate
physical threat.

This design fixes the story's form, dramatic engine, character functions,
temporal rules, epistemic discipline, and ending. It deliberately leaves exact
Permian organisms, palaeogeography, environmental mechanisms, historical
nomenclature, and scene-level incident for the later research dossier and
detailed architecture.

## Relationship To Existing Work

This design depends upon the established *Time Expedition* foundations:

- *The Time Machine* is the sole story-world canon and primary voice anchor.
- Other Wells works contribute only within their declared analytical roles.
- Phase 2B supplies the approved descriptive evidence profile of Wells's
  late-1890s fictional practice.
- Phase 2C will translate that evidence into operational writing guidance and
  style-drift checks.
- Later adaptations supply no characters, technology, events, or continuity.

The story design does not revise the Phase 2B evidence layer. It gives Phase
2C and the Permian research dossier a concrete narrative target.

## Form And Scale

The provisional title is *The Last Week of the Permian*.

The story will use a five-act concentric-testimony structure and target
approximately 18,000 to 24,000 words. It is longer than a conventional short
story, shorter than *The Time Machine*, and substantial enough to support a
gradual scientific clue ladder, a developed survival climax, and a serious
public examination.

The act allocations below total approximately 18,000 to 22,000 words. The
remaining allowance is contingency that may be distributed where research and
scene planning reveal a genuine need; it does not imply another act or a
required expansion.

The manuscript is one standalone expedition within an anthology. It may
contribute quietly to a later framing spine, but it does not depend upon a
shared anthology arc or advertise a specific sequel.

## Recovered-Manuscript Conceit

The governing authenticity test is whether Wells could plausibly have written
this manuscript in the late 1890s.

The story must therefore preserve:

- Wells's story-world continuity and framing habits;
- the scientific vocabulary, instruments, disputes, and limits of the period;
- disciplined speculation arising from observable evidence;
- the possibility of intelligent but incorrect conclusions;
- social and occupational distinctions among speakers;
- the unevenness and uncertainty appropriate to an authentic period work.

Modern scientific knowledge is a backstage calibration aid. It helps prevent
needless impossibility and supports a coherent catastrophe, but it must not
grant Wells or his characters modern omniscience. A period-authentic error may
remain when it is internally coherent, plausible from contemporary knowledge,
and imaginatively productive.

## Narrative Core

The Time Traveller has returned after the unresolved second departure at the
end of *The Time Machine*. He intended to return earlier, but an anomaly near
his original experiments prevented that arrival and displaced him farther
into the future. His account reconciles the apparent finality of the original
epilogue without erasing it: he returned only after the earlier record had
reason to regard him as lost.

Filby mentions a Royal Society lecture concerning the unexplained termination
of Permian life. The Time Traveller resolves to attempt his first deep journey
into the past. His motives combine:

- scientific investigation of a major geological and biological mystery;
- testing the machine's backward range;
- learning more about the temporal anomaly;
- intellectual vanity and a desire to challenge received explanations;
- a genuine appetite for Haggardian adventure.

The adventure influence governs motive, danger, and expedition shape. It does
not replace Wells's sentence-level voice.

The Traveller aims broadly for the late Permian. He cannot target a particular
year, day, or extinction pulse across such a depth of time. The "last week" is
a retrospective description of the local sequence he experiences and samples.
The story need not assert that a planetary extinction occurred everywhere
within seven days. The Geologist may challenge precisely that inference.

## Governing Questions

The dramatic question is:

> Can the Time Traveller understand enough of the mounting catastrophe to
> escape before the landscape destroys his refuge and closes access to the
> machine?

The intellectual question is:

> How much can disciplined science establish from local, incomplete
> observations made during a rapidly changing event?

The character question is:

> Will an encounter with extinction restrain the Traveller's appetite for
> temporal exploration, or deepen it?

The story answers the character question in divided form. The Traveller
returns humbled about complete explanation and increasingly compelled by the
unknown.

## Spatial And Temporal Rules

### Terrestrial Anchoring

The machine remains attached to the terrestrial frame and, more specifically,
to the crustal tract that will become Richmond. It follows Earth's rotation,
orbit, larger motion, and continental displacement while giving the Traveller
no independent lateral movement.

Ancient Richmond is therefore the transformed ancestral site of his study.
The exact palaeolocation will be researched later. The planned story
environment is a mixed landscape with elevated rock, a river plain, and a
nearby shallow coast or inland sea. This variety permits terrestrial and
aquatic evidence while keeping the principal volcanic influence distant and
inferential.

The attachment mechanism remains unexplained. In the Royal Society
examination, the Astronomer asks why the Traveller does not materialise in
empty space after Earth has moved. The Traveller can report the machine's
observed behaviour and speculate about attachment to terrestrial matter or
gravitation. He cannot solve the physical problem.

### Temporal Targeting

The initial deep-time target is coarse. Once established at the ancient site,
the Traveller can make shorter local traverses to compare successive
conditions. These traverses establish relative sequence more reliably than
absolute geological date.

Temporal sampling remains costly:

- calibration at extreme remove is imperfect;
- repeated passages exhaust the Traveller;
- the machine cannot escape local hazards by moving sideways;
- each stop assumes that the cave still provides occupiable space;
- every additional observation reduces the margin available for retreat.

The exact treatment of material intersection and rematerialisation must be
checked against *The Time Machine* before scene planning. The story requires
danger and uncertainty without inventing a mechanical rule that contradicts
the source.

### The Temporal Anomaly

The anomaly around the Traveller's earlier experiments is a restrained
anthology-wide mystery. It explains the delayed return, motivates further
investigation, and may produce minor calibration irregularities during the
Permian journey.

It does not cause the Permian catastrophe or drive the cave climax. The
environmental and biological pressures remain sufficient on their own. The
anomaly may contribute to the final oblique sign of another projected journey.

## Five-Act Architecture

### Act I: The Study

Target length: approximately 2,500 to 3,000 words.

The anonymous narrator finds the Time Traveller with Filby in the Richmond
study. The Traveller completes his account of what followed the second
departure, including the later future and the temporal obstruction that
prevented his intended return.

Filby mentions a recent lecture on the Permian faunal termination. The
conversation turns from geological uncertainty to the untested backward range
of the machine. Curiosity, rivalry with received opinion, and appetite for
adventure converge. The Traveller prepares a compact Victorian field
expedition: instruments, notebooks, collecting and preserving materials, and
provisions suited to the machine's limited capacity.

The act ends with commitment and departure rather than an extended technical
explanation.

### Act II: The Notebook

Target length: approximately 3,000 to 3,500 words.

The Traveller returns in physical collapse and convalesces for more than a
week. He is initially unable or unwilling to give a sustained account. Filby,
seeking more immediate answers, begins reading the recovered expedition
notebook to the anonymous narrator in the downstairs study.

The notebook records:

- the backward descent and uncertain arrival;
- the Traveller's recovery or "revival";
- first observations of ancient Richmond;
- the effort of dragging the machine into a nearby cave;
- establishment of a base camp and fixed temporal observatory;
- baseline surveys of land, water, vegetation, and animal life;
- deliberate collection and preservation of one complete small animal;
- an early encounter with a large predator that regards him with indifference;
- the first individually explicable irregularities.

Early entries are orderly and procedural. Later entries become shorter as
fatigue, temporal sampling, and environmental disturbance increase. The
notebook stops before it can supply a coherent account of the crisis.

The restored Traveller enters unexpectedly. Rather than delivering the full
story privately, he announces that the Royal Society is the proper venue and
asks Filby to arrange an hour for his address. Whether Filby's reading was
technically authorised is a minor scene-level choice; it must create curiosity
and mild social friction rather than a breach that damages their relationship.

### Act III: The Last Week

Target length: approximately 8,000 to 9,500 words.

The Traveller begins his Royal Society address. A short narrative bridge
prevents repetition of notebook material. Once he reaches the unreported
events, the hall recedes and his first-person account becomes immersive in the
manner of the original novella.

The Traveller uses the cave as shelter, storehouse, laboratory, and temporal
station. Repeated short traverses let him compare the same instruments,
shoreline, waterways, vegetation, and animal populations at successive
intervals.

The investigation moves through a cumulative clue ladder:

1. Physical irregularities recur and intensify.
2. Aquatic and terrestrial organisms show distinct forms of stress.
3. Prey animals migrate, weaken, or disappear.
4. The large predator's indifference changes to watchfulness and then
   catastrophe-driven desperation.
5. Temporal comparisons reveal directional collapse rather than ordinary
   local variation.
6. Distant volcanic influence becomes a strong hypothesis through cumulative
   indirect evidence.
7. The cave's apparent geological stability fails under the worsening
   conditions.

Exact symptoms and causal links will be selected through the research dossier.
Candidates include atmospheric discoloration, unusual heat, tremors, altered
water, deposits, vegetation damage, and disordered animal movement. The final
sequence must use a scientifically and historically defensible subset rather
than accumulating every possible disaster effect.

The predator remains an animal rather than a villain. It initially has no
reason to recognise the Traveller as prey or threat. Ecological collapse,
hunger, displacement, and physical stress eventually force it toward risks it
would ordinarily avoid.

The climax joins the story's pressures at the cave. Environmental disturbance
drives the predator into or toward the refuge while structural failure
obstructs access to the machine. The Traveller must recover it, clear enough
space to operate it, and abandon much of the evidence he hoped to retain. He
escapes with the expedition notebook, a compact sample set, and the complete
zoological specimen.

The cave's exact mode of failure will be chosen after geological research. It
must arise from the established catastrophe and cave conditions rather than
from an unrelated last-minute hazard.

### Act IV: The Examination

Target length: approximately 3,000 to 4,000 words.

The story returns fully to the Royal Society room. The audience grants
provisional rather than unanimous acceptance.

A minor gadfly initially disputes the entire premise. Filby, the anonymous
narrator, and any other source-confirmed witnesses can attest to earlier
demonstrations and departures. The machine's established existence, notebook,
instrument records, sample sequence, and specimen form a cumulative
evidentiary case. No single exhibit supplies miraculous proof, and no public
temporal demonstration is required. Historical scene planning will decide
whether the full machine can credibly be inspected at the venue or remains at
Richmond.

The serious examination is conducted through occupational identities:

- the Geologist is the principal intellectual antagonist and tests dating,
  stratigraphy, local-to-global inference, and causal claims;
- the Astronomer exposes the unresolved spatial-anchoring problem;
- the Comparative Anatomist examines the complete animal and its relation to
  known fossil forms;
- the Chemist or natural philosopher tests the atmospheric, water, deposit,
  and volcanic inferences.

Each substantial question must add information, expose a limit, or force the
Traveller to revise the confidence of a claim. The examination cannot become
an encyclopaedic appendix or a sequence of easy victories.

The Traveller distinguishes:

- what he directly observed;
- what the temporal sequence supports;
- what he considers probable;
- what one locality cannot establish.

The Geologist remains unconvinced by some global and causal conclusions while
treating the physical evidence seriously. The scene closes with the
Traveller's credibility largely established and his ultimate interpretation
still contestable.

### Act V: Smoking Bishop

Target length: approximately 1,500 to 2,000 words.

The narrative returns to the Richmond study, cigars, and smoking bishop. Filby
and the Traveller consider the lecture away from its public performance.

The expedition has sobered the Traveller. He recognises that careful evidence
can remain radically incomplete and that a local witness may never settle a
planetary event. The successful journey has also intensified his appetite for
temporal exploration.

The final image is oblique. Filby notices new calculations, altered settings,
or another restrained sign of preparation. No destination is named. The story
ends with renewed purpose and the temporal anomaly still unresolved.

## Evidence And Exhibits

The lecture builds credibility cumulatively:

- prior witnesses and the known machine establish the possibility of temporal
  displacement;
- the notebook and instrument records establish an internally consistent
  sequence;
- compact samples of air, water, deposits, and vegetation document change;
- one complete preserved animal gives the vanished landscape a disturbing
  physical presence.

The zoological specimen distinguishes this expedition from the flowers in
*The Time Machine*. It is collected deliberately during the calmer phase and
is not a trophy from the final predator encounter. Its exact identity,
preservation method, and relationship to fossils known in the 1890s are
research decisions.

The escape must impose evidentiary loss. The Traveller cannot return with a
perfect archive of the catastrophe. The surviving selection supports serious
inquiry while preserving uncertainty.

## Narrative Authority And Voice

Every ancient scene reaches the reader through a period witness. There is no
modern omniscient account and no authorial confirmation of the Traveller's
ultimate explanation.

### Anonymous Narrator

The anonymous narrator controls the outer frame. His prose is restrained,
socially observant, and occasionally ironic. He witnesses the study scenes,
Filby's notebook reading, the Society address, the examination, and the final
coda.

### Filby

Filby remains an active interlocutor rather than a Watson-like narrator. He
provokes the expedition, seeks the notebook account, arranges the lecture,
supplies practical and social pressure, and tests the Traveller privately.

### Expedition Notebook

The notebook is immediate, compressed, and procedural. Its changing density
registers the Traveller's worsening condition. It supplies raw observations
and initial experience without duplicating the later lecture.

### Time Traveller

The lecture sustains the Traveller's reported first-person voice. It combines
exact observation, intellectual confidence, provisional reasoning, flashes of
vanity, and appetite for adventure. The principal narrative should feel lived
rather than like visibly transcribed minutes.

### Society Questioners

The questioners are identified through occupations rather than real
historical celebrity names. Their diction, syntax, and priorities must differ
by discipline. Their function is to test the evidence, not to decorate the
room with period references.

## Scientific And Historical Discipline

Research and drafting will use the following priority order:

1. Wellsian authorship authenticity.
2. Late-1890s epistemic authenticity.
3. Internal scientific coherence.
4. Modern scientific plausibility.

The research dossier will distinguish:

- the best current reconstruction;
- evidence and concepts available in the late 1890s;
- terminology a contemporary speaker could use;
- a plausible Wellsian speculative choice;
- any intentional divergence from modern understanding;
- the observation or argument presented to the reader.

Major story phenomena should be recorded through five questions:

1. What is physically happening under the selected reconstruction?
2. What can the Traveller observe or measure?
3. How could he name it in period terms?
4. What explanation could he plausibly advance?
5. How could a relevant Society specialist challenge it?

Modern accuracy is not a perfection target. It is a guardrail against
accidental incoherence and a way to make historical divergences deliberate.
The story may preserve conclusions that modern readers know to be incomplete
or wrong when those conclusions strengthen the recovered-manuscript conceit.

## Style Boundary

Drafting must avoid:

- a modern hard-SF narrator wearing Victorian costume;
- generic archaism or ornamental "Victorian" prose;
- steampunk conventions;
- disaster-film escalation;
- uniform formal cadence across all speakers;
- modern terminology inserted without a period process of observation,
  analogy, error, and inference;
- later-adaptation inventions;
- explanatory passages that settle what the evidence should leave uncertain.

Phase 2C will convert the Phase 2B evidence into positive operational guidance,
reference-packet rules, and bounded drift checks. The original Wells texts
remain the authority when a heuristic conflicts with authentic source
practice.

## Completion Criteria

The eventual story design and manuscript are successful when:

1. The text reads as a credible late-1890s Wells manuscript.
2. The concentric frames remain clear and preserve narrative momentum.
3. Scientific detection develops naturally into physical survival.
4. Temporal mobility improves investigation while retaining material cost and
   danger.
5. The predator's changing behaviour follows ecological pressure.
6. The notebook creates immediacy without duplicating the lecture.
7. The lecture immerses the reader rather than reading as minutes.
8. The examination materially qualifies the Traveller's account.
9. Observation, inference, and speculation remain distinguishable.
10. Period-authentic scientific errors are permitted and documented.
11. The cave climax resolves immediate danger while leaving causal and
    temporal questions open.
12. The final study scene leaves the Traveller chastened, energised, and
    pointed obliquely toward further travel.
13. The completed story falls within the flexible 18,000 to 24,000-word range.

## Deferred Research Decisions

The following are intentionally unresolved until the Permian dossier:

- the palaeogeography and latitude of Richmond's crustal tract;
- the feasibility and geology of the cave and neighbouring coast;
- local plant and animal communities;
- the identity and 1890s name of the preserved small animal;
- the identity, ecology, and period description of the large predator;
- the selected environmental symptoms and their timing;
- the relationship between local events and wider volcanism;
- period instruments, sample containers, and preservation methods;
- the Society's venue, procedure, and plausible disciplinary vocabulary;
- source-consistent details of short temporal traverses and rematerialisation;
- the exact evidence the Traveller saves or loses during escape.

These are bounded research questions, not invitations to reopen the approved
five-act architecture.

## Non-Goals

This design does not:

- draft or revise manuscript prose;
- prescribe exact Permian science before research;
- implement Phase 2C;
- create the Permian research dossier;
- decompose the provisional manuscript into final chapters or scene cards;
- design the anthology's complete framing spine;
- name or foreshadow the next expedition;
- resolve the temporal anomaly;
- modernise Wells's language, assumptions, or scientific limitations.
