The last clean track disappeared under a skin of drifted dust.

After that, Basalt Ridge had to be read rather than crossed.

Mei brought Mule Three down to walking pace and let the rover's suspension talk
to her through the seat. The wheels climbed over low shelves of broken basalt,
dropped into old contraction cracks, rose again. Swift swept ahead in shallow
arcs, painting the slope with lidar and thermal. Idris kept feeding the rover
micro-corrections from the archive layer, less because the legacy map was good
out here than because he wanted the old machine choices beside the new human
ones in real time.

The ridge face rose dark and columned above them, its fracture geometry almost
too regular until the details asserted themselves: broken terminations, angled
falls, dust tongues collected in lee pockets. Mei liked basalt country because
it rewarded attention without pretending to romance. Every surface had a history
of stress.

"There," Idris said.

Swift had paused in hover against a shoulder of rock thirty metres ahead. On
Mei's visor a cut opened in the ridge: narrow at the mouth, wider in thermal
shadow behind it. Not a skylight. A lateral void, perhaps a lava-tube branch,
perhaps only an overhang deep enough to trap cold.

Mei parked the rover in the lee of a low rise and they stepped out together,
boots sinking less than a centimetre into the mixed dust and grit. The ground
here had the dry, armoured feel of a surface that wind had worked for a very
long time without ever quite mastering it. Their suit fans and the faint hiss
of oxygen were suddenly the only weather.

"Cave threshold at twenty-three metres," Idris said. "Gradient manageable."

Manageable, Mei thought, had become his favourite evasive word.

She walked ahead of him with the sampling wand extended, more to give her hand
something to do than because she expected the instrument to settle the question.
Mars in the open almost always resolved into ordinary explanation if you got
close enough. The problem was that ordinary explanations sometimes killed
people.

At twelve metres from the opening she stopped.

The mark on the basalt was only six centimetres long. In the visor glare it
might have been a narrow shadow, but at angle it resolved into an incision:
clean-sided, shallow, almost parallel to the ground. Mei bent close enough for
her helmet lights to rake across it. A second cut lay thirty centimetres above
the first. A third under a lip of rock, half filled with dust.

"Idris."

He came alongside and crouched. "Anchor scoring?"

"From what?"

He ran his gloved hand a careful centimetre above the rock without touching it.
"Crawler spike. Tool slip. Survey piton."

Mei measured spacing with the wand. The cuts were similar in depth and width,
but they did not line up with any wheelbase or articulated reach pattern she
had seen in the Argosy documentation. More important, they sat in sheltered
positions, tucked under lips and inside minor wind shadows where abrasion would
preserve them while stripping everything else. They had lasted.

"These are deliberate," she said.

"Deliberate does not mean mysterious."

"No. It means deliberate."

She widened the search. Once her eye had the pattern, more marks emerged:
single incisions on adjacent stones, some nearly erased, some sharp. The cave
mouth itself was a dark cleft three metres high with a spill of collapsed blocks
below it. On the right side, a narrow shelf ran inward. On the left, wind had
stacked red dust against black rock until the colour boundary looked painted.

Idris checked the archive layer again and was silent for a few seconds too long.

"The omission line is exact," he said at last. "It begins eleven metres west of
the mouth."

Mei looked up. "Not a failed survey, then."

"Not if the archive we have is honest."

"And if it is not?"

"Then I have bigger theological problems than you do."

Nadiya's voice cut in from the colony with the flat compression of local relay.
"Status."

Mei stood and turned her helmet cam toward the opening. "We have a real void.
Likely lava-tube branch. More importantly, the inherited map omission is
intentional. Surface scoring present around the mouth, inconsistent with simple
wind abrasion."

"Natural fracture?"

"Possible for the opening. Not for the cuts."

Nadiya absorbed that without drama. "Can you classify hazard from outside?"

"No."

"Then go a little way inside and stop before bravery starts."

Idris said, "That may be the most useful operational doctrine anyone has yet
written on this planet."

Nadiya ignored him. "Log every metre. If this turns out to be nothing, I still
want the route. If it turns out to be something, I want enough evidence to hold
eastward works by local authority."

Mei acknowledged and muted the channel for a moment. "She is in a generous
mood."

"She is in a realistic one."

They advanced to the threshold. Temperature dropped two degrees the instant the
sun left Mei's visor. The opening swallowed light fast. Basalt ribs curved over
them where the old melt had drained and cooled, leaving a tube with a partially
collapsed side chamber at the mouth. The floor was not smooth. It was broken
slab, dust pockets, and frozen-looking dribbles of darker glass where later
flows had slicked the wall.

Swift descended and slipped through the opening ahead of them, its rotors
reduced to a cautious murmur. Its lidar threw a wireframe into Mei's display: a
chamber twenty metres in, then a narrowing turn to the right and a deeper void
beyond the first wall. The geometry had the recognisable indifference of lava
work, but there were interruptions in it: low stacks of dust, small reflective
points at ankle height, something rectangular tucked behind a rock lip on the
right-hand wall.

Mei crouched first.

The object was half buried in dust held together by static and cold. She used
the wand to brush around it until the edges came free: a thin lozenge of
ceramic-glass composite, pale under the dust, no bigger than her palm. One face
had a shallow stamped mark in it, almost erased. Idris made a sound she had not
heard from him before, something between satisfaction and alarm.

"What is it?"

"Argosy witness tag."

He took it from the dust with exaggerated care and turned it under his lights.
Now Mei saw the mark too: three nested angles around a central point. Machine
symbolics had become almost decorative by the time humans arrived at Ares, but
the early construction fleet used a stripped working set for field conditions.
Mei did not know the lexicon. Idris did.

"Survey complete?" she asked.

"More specific. Witness logged. Usually means a machine inspected an anomaly and
left a local marker to connect the place to a maintenance record."

"Usually logged where?"

"In the archive," he said.

They looked at each other through two layers of visor reflection.

Mei said, "And this one is not."

Idris already had a power lead out from his suit utility panel. "It may be too
dead to say anything."

"Do it anyway."

He seated the contact, and for three long seconds nothing happened. Then the tag
threw a weak amber pulse and a fragment of data ghosted across Idris's sleeve
screen: corrupted header, construction-layer timestamp, local coordinate hash,
then a single intact line before the voltage dropped again.

SITE HELD

"Held how?" Mei said.

"No idea. That is all we got."

Nadiya came back onto the open channel as Idris transmitted the fragment. There
was a brief silence at her end, perhaps not thought so much as the time it took
for the colony's legal and operational layers to search for a category large
enough to contain the message.

"If a construction marker says hold," she said, "I need to know if the reason is
structural, chemical, topographic, or something stranger. Can you proceed
another ten to fifteen metres safely?"

Mei swept the chamber again with her lamps. The first room widened to the right,
then narrowed into a throat. On the floor, dust had settled into faint arcs as
if old air or old movement had patterned it. Along the wall at shin height,
three small metal glints resolved into flush-mounted discs no natural cave ever
grew.

"Yes," she said. "Safely enough."

Idris was already at the first wall disc, clearing dust with a brush from his
kit. "Passive repeater housing," he said. "Dead. Maybe part of a breadcrumb
trail."

"For what?"

"For machines that expected to come back."

That was the moment the cave stopped being a question of geology and became a
question of attention. The Argosy fleet had not glanced at this place and moved
on. It had entered, marked, instrumented, and then removed the fact of doing so
from the layer of the world the humans were now using.

Mei moved deeper into the chamber.

Her boots disturbed a skin of dust over harder floor. Under it, a faint smooth
strip ran inward across the broken rock, no wider than a man's shoulders and too
regular to be a natural wear pattern. Machine traffic, light and repeated. The
wireframe from Swift bent right through the throat ahead, then opened again into
something larger.

"Power and turnback?" she asked.

Idris checked. "Still inside margin."

Nadiya said, "You get one deeper chamber. Then I want the hold call from actual
evidence, not instinct."

Mei almost told her that instinct was what kept actual evidence from killing
people. She did not. Nadiya knew.

Instead she said, "Understood."

The throat narrowed them to single file. Mei went first, one hand on the wall,
lights scanning over basalt that had flowed once like thick liquid and now held
its last movement forever. Twice she saw more cuts, the same shallow incisions
as outside, tucked into places only deliberate eyes would think to look. Once
she found a patch of wall polished dull and smooth, as if a machine body had
passed the same angle more times than it strictly needed.

Then the passage opened and Swift's wireframe bloomed into a wider room ahead.

In Mei's lights, dust lay on the floor in scalloped drifts around a low object
on the far side. It looked at first like a fallen boulder, then like a piece of
equipment, then like neither. Beside it, the wall carried a run of old mounting
points and one intact service node.

Idris came up on her right and spoke very softly, as if the cave were listening
through the basalt.

"That," he said, "is not a place the fleet visited once."
