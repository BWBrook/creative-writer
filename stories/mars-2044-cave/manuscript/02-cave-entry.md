At twenty metres range Swift's first thermal sweep resolved the seam in the
ridge into a cavity.

The temperature contrast was modest, 3.8 kelvins below the adjacent sunlit
basalt, but it persisted after the rest of the slope had begun to equalise. That
was enough to classify a void and not enough to classify the void as safe.

Mei parked Mule Three in the lee of a low basalt rise and checked the gradient
to the opening. Fourteen degrees at the steepest part of the approach. Walkable
in suit. The ridge shoulder above them was columnar in the old lava geometry,
but broken by later collapse and by long narrow shelves where dust had collected
out of the wind.

"Threshold at twenty-three metres," Idris said. "Roofline at roughly three
metres. Scout returns suggest an initial chamber at twenty metres depth."

"Confidence?"

"Sixty-four per cent on chamber geometry. Less on what caused it."

They left the rover with Swift holding station above the opening and walked in
with suit lamps low. The ground near the mouth was armoured dust over broken
basalt, compact enough to take shallow prints and keep them. Mei was looking at
the fracture line when a small discontinuity in the rock caught the light.

She stopped at once.

The first incision was only six centimetres long. In flat light it disappeared.
At angle it showed a cut surface: shallow, clean-sided, almost horizontal. Mei
shifted half a step left and found a second above it, then a third under a wind
lip where ordinary abrasion would not have preserved an accidental mark.

"Idris."

He came up beside her and crouched. "Tool contact?"

"Measure it."

He put a scale bar down without touching the rock. Mei brought up the image on
her wrist display and took the width from the enlarged view.

"Four millimetres," she said. "Nearly constant. Depth about the same."

Idris considered the line and then the opening. "A crawler spike shoe is wider.
A survey piton would leave radial fracture. This does neither."

Mei widened the inspection. Once she had the pattern, more cuts became visible
on adjacent stones, usually in lee positions or under minor overhangs. That
mattered. A random scrape on open basalt would have weathered at the same rate
as the surface around it. These had been placed where time could not erase them
quickly.

"Deliberate," she said.

"Agreed," Idris said. "Not yet classified."

He checked the archive overlay again. This time he did not try to soften the
result.

"The omission begins eleven metres west of the mouth," he said. "The route layer
clips around it exactly."

"Then the fleet reached this point and withheld it."

"Or its safety layer did."

Nadiya came up on comms. "Report."

Mei turned her helmet camera towards the opening. "Confirmed void in ridge
sector E-54. Surface shows repeated incisions inconsistent with wind abrasion or
ordinary vehicle contact. Archive omission begins at the cave mouth, not at a
distance from it."

"Can you classify hazard from outside?"

"No."

"Then proceed one chamber. No cutting. No sampling beyond loose surface dust.
Bring me enough to decide whether eastward works stop here."

The order was exact and adequate. Mei acknowledged and left the channel open.
There was no advantage in pretending the colony was not with them.

At the threshold the temperature fell by 2.1 kelvins and the light shortened
abruptly. The opening was a lateral cut into the ridge, likely a branch of an
older lava tube partly collapsed at the mouth. The floor was broken slab and
finer dust, with glassier dribbles on the wall where late-stage melt had frozen
into place. Swift lowered itself and advanced ahead on reduced rotor power,
feeding lidar into Mei's visor.

The wireframe showed a chamber twenty metres in, then a narrowing throat to the
right and a deeper return beyond the first wall. Nothing in the geometry itself
was strange. Lava makes rooms like this. The strange parts were the local
interruptions: a row of reflective points at shin height, a rectilinear object
behind a rock lip, a smooth strip on the floor where dust had not accumulated as
it should.

Mei went first to the rectilinear object.

It lay half buried in electrostatically bound dust. She cleared it with the tip
of the sampling wand until a ceramic-glass lozenge came free, pale under the
coating and no larger than her palm. One face carried a stamped symbol nearly
erased by time.

Idris took it carefully. "Argosy witness tag," he said.

The early construction fleet had used local markers when a machine inspected an
anomaly and wanted the site tied to a maintenance record even if the main relay
was down. The symbol on the tag was from that older field set: three nested
angles around a point.

"And it should appear where?" Mei asked.

"In the archive," Idris said.

It did not.

He took a power lead from his suit utility panel, cleaned the contact edge, and
fed a low charge into the tag. On the third second the marker gave a weak amber
pulse and printed a fragment across his sleeve display.

> ARGOSY FIELD TAG
> STATUS: SITE HELD
> RECORD: CORRUPTED

The charge failed at once.

"That is all?" Mei said.

"That is all that survived locally."

Nadiya received the fragment a few seconds later. When she came back on the
channel her tone had tightened by a fraction. "If the construction fleet held
the site, I need to know whether the reason was structural, chemical or
operational. Can you advance safely?"

Mei swept the chamber in a slow circle. Along the wall at shin height three dead
discs sat flush in the basalt.

"Those are repeaters," Idris said before she asked. "Local breadcrumb units.
Short range. Low power."

"How many visits does that imply?"

"More than one."

That altered the status of the cave. This was no longer a topographic omission.
The fleet had entered, instrumented and revisited the site. The absence in the
human map layer was the residue of that decision.

Mei walked the smooth strip on the floor. Under the dust the basalt had been
abraded or compacted in a line about a metre wide, too regular for natural wear.
Light machine traffic, repeated enough to hold a path.

"Power and turnback?" she asked.

Idris checked the suit timer and rover reserve. "Nominal return remains 4 h 56
m. Hard turnback 3 h 16 m. One deeper chamber is safe if we stop there."

Nadiya said, "Then take the deeper chamber and bring me evidence, not
interpretation."

The passage beyond narrowed them to single file. Mei went first, one hand on the
wall. Twice she found more of the same incisions, tucked into protected angles.
Once she found a patch of basalt polished dull and smooth at shoulder height, as
if a machine body or manipulator arm had passed the same point again and again.

Then the passage opened and the marker room appeared in Swift's wireframe.

In Mei's lights, dust lay on the floor in scalloped drifts around a low object
on the far side. It looked at first like a fallen boulder, then like a piece of
equipment, then like neither. Beside it, the wall carried a run of old mounting
points and one intact service node.

Idris came up on her right and spoke very softly, as if the cave were listening
through the basalt.

"That," he said, "is not a place the fleet visited once."
