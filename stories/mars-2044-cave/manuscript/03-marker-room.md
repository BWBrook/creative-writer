The service node took power on the second attempt.

Idris cleaned the contact face with a fibre pick, warmed the seal until the
housing relaxed, and fed low current from his suit pack into the dead unit. It
sat at knee height in the wall: an ungainly Argosy field device intended to keep
local memory when the main relay path failed. Above them Swift held station and
mapped the chamber in green wireframe.

The room was natural in origin and artificial in use. It had begun as a widened
section of lava tube. The fleet had imposed order on it afterwards. A smooth
track crossed the floor to the wall node and to the dark object beyond. Dust had
collected along the margins in crescents, leaving the centre lane cleaner than
the rest. Three dead repeaters in the wall marked a short-range network line.

"If this is only a survey station," Mei said, "the handover archive is missing a
significant layer."

"Yes," Idris said. "That would be the smaller problem."

The node changed from red to amber. Static cracked once across the comm loop and
then steadied. Idris opened the directory tree and found a field cache,
part-corrupted but not empty.

"Decision log intact enough to read," he said.

He opened it and pushed the text to both visors.

> ARGOSY CONSTRUCTION SAFETY CACHE
> SITE HELD
> HAZARD CLASS UNKNOWN
> NO CUTTING
> ROUTE REVISED
> LOCAL AUTHORITY: SAFETY LAYER 4
> RELAY: DEFERRED DURING CONJUNCTION
> REVISIT: ACTIVE
> CONFIDENCE: 0.42

Mei read the sequence twice. Route revised. That was the crucial line. The blank
band on the human map was not a data failure. The fleet had reached this sector,
made a local safety decision and diverted later route planning around it. During
solar conjunction the site relay had been deferred. At some later stage the
civilian settlement abstraction had kept the revised routes and dropped the
reason.

Nadiya came onto the channel as soon as the log reached operations. "Confidence
forty-two per cent," she said. "Low for a verdict. High enough for caution."

"Agreed," Mei said.

"Caution against what?" Nadiya asked.

Idris was already pulling up the image bundles. "That part remains unresolved."

The first recovered frame was useless. The second survived long enough to matter.
It showed the chamber years earlier through the crawler's poorer optics: the
track on the floor less dusted, the wall node alive, the chamber geometry the
same. On the far side of the room, however, an upright object stood in the place
where Mei had taken a dark mass for stone.

She said, "Freeze."

Idris froze the image and enlarged it.

The object was about forty centimetres high and narrow in section. The crawler
in the present chamber lay on its side facing the same point.

"The fleet did not make that," Mei said.

Idris did not answer immediately. His argument with the cave had narrowed over
the last twenty minutes from theory to evidence, and evidence was winning. "No,"
he said. "It found it."

Mei crossed the chamber towards the object. The crawler on the floor was an
early Argosy utility body: six radial legs, mast snapped away, hull scarred on
one side by heat or impact. A witness, not a culprit. Beyond it stood the
upright piece itself, set in a shallow notch in the floor.

At two metres she could see that it was basalt, or basalt altered by heat but
still local in composition. The exposed face was smoother than the chamber wall
and slightly glossy. Six parallel incisions ran down it. She measured them with
her scale overlay: each cut roughly eleven millimetres from the next, lengths
within a centimetre of one another, depths sufficient to hold dust and salt.
That was too regular for fracture and too simple for ornament as most humans
understood ornament.

She circled it without touching.

"No fasteners," she said. "No tool flats. No alloy residue. If it is fabricated,
the material came from here."

Idris compared the cuts to the witness tag symbol set. "Not Argosy notation. Not
any field safety code I know."

"Could the crawler have fused it in situ?"

"If it had, the cache would contain a creation path. Instead it contains a hold
order."

Behind the slab the wall curved inward to a darker seam, a zone where basalt,
salts and glassy film had collected together. Thin frost persisted in the more
protected grooves. Swift's thermal overlay showed the seam running a few tenths
of a kelvin warmer than the surrounding wall.

Mei moved closer. Some parts of the film were translucent at the edge. Within it
lay cloudy tubular inclusions, perhaps vapour pathways, perhaps nothing more than
mineral structure. In another setting she would have begun a cautious argument
about possible biosignature preservation. Here the presence of the slab and the
machine hold order made caution mandatory before inference.

"Thermal differential?" she asked.

"Point three kelvin above wall mean," Idris said. "Could be salt hydration. Could
be residual subsurface heat transport. Not enough for a conclusion."

Nadiya said, "Enough for a local stop on heavy cutting?"

Mei did not answer at once. She photographed the seam, then the slab, then the
cuts around its base. Some older incisions had partly filled with dust. One had
been crossed by later mineral growth. The chronology was not obvious, but the
place had a history of repeated marking.

"Possibly," she said. "I want the chamber beyond this one before I give you the
final recommendation."

Idris checked Swift's map. "There is another chamber. Narrow slot. Two metres at
least, perhaps three."

"Power margin?" Nadiya asked.

"Nominal return 4 h 11 m," Idris said. "Hard turnback 2 h 31 m. One more chamber
is within the plan."

She stood.

"One more chamber," she said.

Idris copied the surviving cache into his suit memory and looked once at the
broken crawler. "There is an odd dignity to this," he said. "The first thing
from Earth to reach the site was a machine. The first thing from Earth to admit
it did not understand the site was also a machine."

Mei looked towards the narrow bend beyond the warm seam.

"Then we should try to be at least as careful," she said.
