The intact service node took power on the second attempt.

Idris had to strip dust from the contacts with a fibre pick and warm the housing
with the back of his gloved hand until the cold-brittle seal relaxed. The node
sat at knee height in the wall, one of the small ugly devices the construction
fleet had used when it needed field memory more than elegance. Mei stood over
him while Swift held station above the chamber floor and raked the room in thin
green wireframe.

Calling it a room was itself a concession to intention. The chamber was natural
in origin, a widened section of tube where the old melt had slowed and pooled.
Nothing human or machine had made its shape. Yet the Argosy fleet had imposed a
way of using it. The smooth strip across the floor led directly to the node and
to the dark object beyond. Dust had collected around the margins in crescents,
leaving the centre path cleaner than the rest. Three dead repeaters sat in the
rock like a line of careful breadcrumbs.

"Any chance this is just an abandoned survey station?" Mei asked.

Idris kept his attention on the node. "If it is, the archive we inherited has a
hole in it large enough to drive the colony through."

The node blinked from red to amber. A brittle burst of static cut across their
comms, then steadied. Idris gave a short satisfied nod. "Come on," he said to
the machine, as if stubbornness might still be the right language. "You owe us a
file."

Mei angled her lights toward the object at the far end of the chamber while he
worked. From here it resolved into a low machine frame lying half on its side,
no larger than a goat and nearly coated in dust: six radial legs, sensor mast
snapped off, one side melted or abraded into a dull curve. A crawler, early
Argosy generation by the look of it. Old enough that its body shape still tried
to look general-purpose instead of specialised.

Not dead here by accident, then. Dead in witness of something.

Idris's screen brightened. A local directory tree appeared, ragged with missing
blocks. He scrolled with the focused impatience of a man trying not to appear
excited. "Maintenance layer. Field anomaly cache. Two image bundles corrupted,
one partially readable. Decision log intact enough to be annoying."

"Read it."

He opened the first intact entry and the words arrived with old machine
severity, formatted for no human eye in particular:

SITE HELD. HAZARD CLASS UNKNOWN. NO CUTTING. ROUTE REVISED.

Below it were fields for authority, confidence, revisit interval, and relay
status.

LOCAL AUTHORITY: CONSTRUCTION SAFETY LAYER 4.
RELAY: DEFERRED DURING CONJUNCTION.
REVISIT: ACTIVE.

Mei read it twice. "Route revised."

"Which answers one question," Idris said.

It did. The missing strip on the human maps had not been oversight. The Argosy
fleet had reached the site, classified it as something that should not be cut,
and altered the eastward route planning around it. The archive layer handed to
the humans had retained the revised routes and shed the reason, either through
later abstraction or because no one on Earth had thought a local field hold in
an unused corridor needed a red flag seven years later on another world.

The effect was the same. The colony had inherited the decision and lost the
argument.

Nadiya came on the channel without being asked. Idris had already pushed the log
fragment back toward the colony. "I have it," she said. "How much of that
confidence score survived?"

"Forty-two percent," Idris said. "Low enough to classify, high enough to route
around."

"Which sounds like the machine equivalent of caution."

Mei kept looking at the line that mattered. No cutting. "Caution against what?"

Idris dug into the next file. "Maybe structure. Maybe chemistry. Maybe thermal
instability. Give me the image bundle."

The first recovered image was mostly noise, sensor scatter across a close wall.
The second held for longer before corruption ate it. Mei saw the chamber as the
crawler had seen it years earlier: lower resolution, harsher contrast, geometry
without sentiment. The smooth floor strip was newer then, less dusted. The node
glowed live in the wall. On the far side of the room, something stood upright on
the chamber floor.

Not the crawler. Not the node.

Mei stepped closer to Idris until their shoulders nearly touched. "Freeze that."

He did.

The object in the image was no more than forty centimetres high, edge-on to the
camera. Triangular in stance. Dark. It occupied almost the same point on the
floor as the shape Mei had mistaken, on first glance, for stone.

The crawler in the present chamber lay on its side facing it.

"So the fleet did not make that," Mei said.

Idris did not answer at once. It was the first time since leaving the colony
that he had looked as if he might prefer the cave to be ordinary and knew it no
longer would be. "No," he said finally. "It found it."

That sentence altered the whole chamber.

Before, Mei had been willing to treat the site as a machine story, strange only
because the humans had inherited it in fragments. After the image, the Argosy
fleet became the second actor. The cave kept its first.

She crossed the room slowly toward the upright object while Idris followed with
the camera running. Dust rose from each footfall in brief copper clouds and
settled again. The chamber wall behind the object curved inward to a darker seam
where salt and glass had filmed the basalt in a dull mineral sheen. Thin frost
held in protected grooves. The air inside Mei's suit tasted faintly metallic
through the scrubber, a ghost of her own nerves.

At two metres out, the object stopped pretending to be a stone.

It was made of basalt, or had once been. A narrow slab of fused dark material
stood on edge in a shallow notch in the floor as if it had been set there
deliberately. One face was smoother than the chamber walls and slightly glossy,
as if heat had reflowed the rock. Across that face ran six parallel incisions,
cut deep enough to catch dust and pale salts. They were evenly spaced and of
nearly equal length. They did not look like script. They looked like an act
repeated until precision replaced flourish.

Mei circled left without touching it. The base was partly buried in dust and a
friable ring of mineral deposit. No obvious tool marks on the edges. No fastener
points. No alloy reflection.

"Local material," she said. "Or local enough to imitate it."

Idris compared the incisions against the witness tag symbol set on his sleeve.
"Not Argosy notation. Not any safety-layer shorthand I know. Too regular for
fracture."

"Could the fleet have fused and cut it with field tools?"

"If it did, why stand a crawler here and write no creation log? Why hold the
site instead of cataloguing the object as work product?"

Because the fleet had not made it, Mei thought, and did not know what to name
the fact of finding it.

She turned her lights toward the wall seam behind the slab. Up close, the dark
film resolved into a narrow band of glassy deposition threaded with salts. Some
parts were opaque, some translucent at the thinnest edges, with cloudy tubular
inclusions trapped inside. They might have been vapour pathways from a mineral
phase change. They might have been nothing. In another setting, on another
planet, a scientist would have argued carefully around the possibility of fossil
textures. Here, with the slab in front of it and the machine hold order in the
wall behind her, the seam felt like the kind of evidence that punished haste.

"Thermal?" she asked.

Idris glanced at Swift's overlay. "A few tenths above the wall mean. Not much.
Could be salt hydration, could be retained subsurface gradient."

Nadiya said, "Could be enough to justify my stopping work?"

Mei did not answer immediately. She crouched near the floor and found more of
the shallow cuts on the basalt around the slab. Some were old, dust-softened.
One cut crossed a mineral skin and had been partly overgrown. Another sat
clearer, sharper. She photographed each with scale markers.

"Maybe," she said at last. "I need the room beyond this one if there is one."

Idris looked up from Swift's map. "There is. Narrow slot behind the seam bend.
Two metres deep at least, maybe more."

Nadiya was silent for a beat. "Power margin."

Idris checked automatically. "Still inside return, though I would stop calling
it comfortable."

Mei kept her eyes on the slab. Six strokes. No alphabet she knew. No geometry
she could dismiss as random once she had seen the spacing. A local object,
apparently older than the machine that found it, standing in a Martian cave the
colony had nearly cut into from above because the inherited route revision had
outlived its own explanation.

This was what the first human occupation of Mars actually looked like, she
thought. Not flags, not speeches. Old machine caution and a thing in the dark
that made caution reasonable.

She stood.

"One more chamber," she said.

Idris closed the node cache, copied the remaining fragments, and took one long
look at the crawler on the floor. "You realise what this means for the archive."

"For the archive?"

"It means the first thing that reached this chamber from Earth was a machine.
The first thing that reached it and knew it had reached something unusual was
also a machine. We are already late to our own mystery."

Mei looked from him to the slab and then to the narrow bend beyond the mineral
seam, where Swift's wireframe showed the cave continuing in a small black hook.

"Late," she said, "is still better than blind."
