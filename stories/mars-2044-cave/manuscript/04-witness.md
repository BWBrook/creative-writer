The passage into the inner chamber forced them to turn sideways.

On Swift's wireframe it had looked like a simple bend. In person it was a
compressed slot behind the mineral seam where a later contraction crack had
opened the tube wall by less than a metre. Mei went first with one shoulder to
the basalt, moving slowly to avoid striking the wall with her life-support pack.
The rock here held the cold differently. Frost lay in the narrowest grooves as a
thin white fur. Her lights picked out old drip textures and a few more of the
same shallow cuts, though these were scarcer and harder to see.

The slot opened after four metres into a chamber no larger than an office and
far quieter than the rooms behind it. Not because the sound changed. The suit
fans still filled Mei's helmet, and Idris still breathed behind her on the
shared channel. The quiet came from the shape of the space. It had the feeling
of somewhere that enclosed its own terms.

The floor was flatter here, crusted in places with pale mineral scale over dark
basalt. The seam from the outer chamber ran across the back wall and widened
into a shallow lens where salts, glass, and something duller had collected in
layers. The upright slab in the previous room had been austere. This chamber was
messier, more physical. The place where material accumulated. The place where
something might have been read as hazard by a cautious machine.

Mei crouched by the back wall and held her gloves just above the lens without
touching it. Embedded in the deposit were thin clouded tubes, some no wider than
a hair, some thicker, all branching or curving in ways that could have been
strictly mineral and still feel intimate to the eye. A faint thermal bloom on
Swift's overlay mapped the same region. A fraction warmer than the surrounding
rock. Enough to keep a cycle going. Enough to invite every disastrous shortcut a
story about Mars ever wanted to take.

She had no intention of taking it.

"Salt lens with possible intermittent brine activity," she said for the record.
"Protected microenvironment. No visible flowing liquid."

Idris squeezed through the slot and came up on her left. "The hold order makes
more sense if the fleet flagged this as a contamination or preservation problem."

"If it knew what it was looking at."

"It did not need to know. It only needed to know cutting it was a bad bet."

Nadiya said, "I am hearing an argument to stop excavation."

Mei straightened and turned to face the chamber as a whole. The scale and the
slab in the outer room wanted to drag her toward the object as the central fact.
The back wall resisted that. The lens of salt and glass, the trapped inclusions,
the protected cold, the long machine caution. The site might be one mystery or
two that only looked like one from the doorway.

"You have that argument already," she said. "I am looking for the shape of the
better one."

Swift drifted higher and mapped the ceiling. There was no hidden branch above
them, no secondary shaft, no obvious place from which equipment or debris could
have fallen. The chamber roof was unhelpfully whole. The floor, however, dipped
slightly toward the back wall, and in that depression lay the finest dust Mei
had yet seen in the cave. It settled differently from the heavier grit near the
entrance, finer and darker, with a texture like ground charcoal.

She knelt again and swept her lights along the floor.

That was where she found the newest cut.

It was only a little longer than the ones outside, perhaps seven centimetres,
and laid at a slight upward angle in the basalt beside the salt lens. Nothing in
the mark announced itself. No glow, no impossible geometry. Its power lay in
its relation to the rest. Dust had not yet softened its edges. The mineral skin
on one side was split cleanly. The incision was narrower than the early crawler
tool marks Idris had shown her in training archives, narrower than anything her
gloved hand could plausibly have made by accident while standing here. It looked
recent. Recent was a dangerous word on Mars. So was fresh. She used neither in
the spoken log.

"New surface incision adjacent to the warm lens," she said instead. "Sharper
than exterior scoring. Width inconsistent with the outer marks."

Idris was beside her immediately. He aimed his own lights in and said nothing
for several seconds. Then, quietly, "That was not in the archive image from the
outer chamber."

"No."

"Could this be a flake line?"

She traced the edges with the wand without touching. "No conchoidal break. No
stress halo. Somebody cut, scratched, or scored this."

"The crawler?"

"Show me how."

He did not answer because he could not. The dead machine in the outer room had
legs and manipulators built for clinging, probing, carrying sensors and laying
field hardware. Its tools were blunt in the archive diagrams, built for work and
survival rather than delicacy. The cut beside the lens had a narrow certainty to
it. It did not look like damage. It looked like a mark placed where a mark
needed to be.

Nadiya's voice came back after a short burst of private conference traffic Mei
could not hear. "I have authority to hold the east corridor on safety grounds if
either of you says the site is not fit for cutting."

Either of you. A wise choice of pronoun. It made the decision a professional
one, not a captain's override or a geologist's instinct.

Idris still stared at the incision. "The fleet revised the route and kept
revisiting. That alone is enough to stop heavy works until we understand the
site."

Mei looked at him. There it was, the first clean surrender he had made to the
cave. Not surrender to superstition, which she would have disliked, but to the
fact that inherited machine judgement had earned a hearing and then exceeded the
records that justified it.

"Agreed," she said.

Nadiya did not waste the moment. "Then I am issuing a local hold on eastward
cutting operations pending scientific review. Mark the site. Bring back every
image and sensor trace you can carry. Do not sample the lens. Do not touch the
object. Do not let this become folklore before it becomes evidence."

Despite herself, Mei smiled. "Understood."

"And Mei."

"Yes."

"You were right to go past the map edge. You will not make me regret saying so."

The channel clicked closed.

For a moment neither Mei nor Idris moved. Outside the chamber, beyond the slot
and the marker room and the threshold, Mars remained precisely what it had been
an hour earlier: cold ground beside a young colony under logistical pressure.
Inside the chamber, a human settlement had just chosen restraint over momentum
without yet knowing what, exactly, it was preserving.

It felt like a beginning much more than a conclusion.

Idris set a temporary beacon at the slot mouth and linked it to the rover. "If
the archive relay had made it through conjunction," he said, "someone on Earth
might have wrapped this in committee seven years ago."

"Would that have helped?"

"It would have meant we were not the first people to read the omission as
absence."

Mei let her lights move once more over the chamber floor. Over the lens. Over
the new incision. Over the layered wall and the dust gathered in the shallow
depression below it. The site did not look staged. That was the worst and best
thing about it. Whatever had happened here had happened in rock, salt, heat,
time, and action. It had entered the world as material before it entered anyone's
story.

She backed into the slot, turned sideways, and paused when her lamps reached the
outer chamber again. From this angle the upright basalt slab stood in profile
against the darker wall beyond, six pale incisions running down its face like a
count, or a warning, or the remains of some simpler act for which human language
had already become too eager.

The dead crawler lay where the first expedition from Earth had left it, angled
toward the slab like a witness under instructions. The service node in the wall
glowed faint amber from Idris's borrowed power, holding its last few fragments
long enough for human eyes. The site had already produced one order from a
machine and one from a settlement. Both orders amounted to the same admission.

Leave it uncut.

Mei turned to go, then stopped.

On the basalt beside the slab, just above the level where the dust thinned into
the smoother floor strip, something caught the low angle of her lamp. She
stepped back and tilted her head until it resolved.

Another incision.

Not old enough to have gathered the same dust skin as the cuts around it. Not
bright enough to be raw from the instant. Simply there, narrow and exact, one
line more than the first quick count had allowed. She could not say whether it
had been hidden by angle the first time she looked, whether the cave had
revealed it only because she now knew the grammar of the place better, or
whether the thought that tried to form next had any right to exist in a
scientist's mind at all.

Idris followed her gaze. "Was that in your first pass?"

Mei kept her voice level with effort. "No."

He looked at the slab, the wall, the mark, then at the chamber behind them as if
distance inside the same cave might still produce a safer explanation.

Mei did not answer. She recorded the new line, transmitted the image to the
colony, and keyed the site beacon to maximum visibility for the next team. Then
she turned away from the slab and led them out.

At the mouth of the chamber she looked back once. The fresh incision held the
low angle of her lamp a fraction longer than the older marks, pale in the
basalt like the start of another count.
