The slot into the inner chamber narrowed to less than a metre.

Mei turned sideways to pass through it, one shoulder against the basalt, careful
not to strike the life-support pack on the rock. Frost persisted in the deepest
grooves. The wall temperature was lower here by nearly a kelvin. The passage
opened after four metres into a chamber no larger than an office and markedly
different from the room behind it.

The floor was flatter. Pale mineral scale had formed in sheets over the basalt.
The warm seam from the marker room widened into a shallow lens at the back wall,
where salts, glassy films and duller deposits had accumulated together. If the
outer room had looked like a site of inspection, this one looked like a site of
process.

Mei crouched by the lens without touching it. Within the deposit lay cloudy
tubular inclusions, some hair-fine, some thicker, curving through the mineral
film in patterns that could have been entirely inorganic. Swift's thermal overlay
showed the same zone running 0.4 kelvin above the chamber wall mean.

"Field note," she said. "Salt lens. Possible intermittent brine history.
Protected microenvironment. No visible flowing liquid."

Idris came through the slot behind her and studied the display. "That alone
could justify the hold order."

"Yes," Mei said. "If the fleet saw it."

"Or if it saw enough to know cutting through the seam was a poor experiment."

Nadiya was listening. "Are you recommending a stop on excavation?"

"I am assembling the argument," Mei said.

That was the correct phrase. A recommendation strong enough to halt trenching on
Sol 11 had to survive more than intuition. It had to survive the operations log,
the safety board, and eventually the people on Earth who would ask why local
authority on Mars had set aside the best expansion corridor on the strength of a
half-corrupted machine cache and a geological anomaly not yet fully described.

Mei surveyed the chamber systematically. Roof intact. No hidden skylight. No
secondary shaft visible from floor level. The fine dust at the base of the warm
lens had settled differently from the coarser material outside, which suggested
quieter air or longer shelter. She took images, thermal frames and close surface
geometry.

That was when she saw the new incision.

It lay beside the warm lens at a slight upward angle, about seven centimetres
long. On the enlarged visor image the edges were sharp enough to split the
mineral skin cleanly. Width: roughly two millimetres. Too narrow for the crawler
tools Idris had shown her in the archive. Too controlled for a simple flake line.

"Record this," she said.

Idris was already at her shoulder. "That was not in the recovered image from the
outer chamber."

"No."

"Natural fracture?"

Mei enlarged the cut again. No conchoidal break. No stress halo. No branching.
"Not a useful one," she said.

She did not say recent. On Mars, where erosion is slow and evidence older than a
human lifetime can remain visually young, recent is a word that requires more
discipline than most people give it.

Nadiya heard the change in their breathing before she heard the explanation.
"What have you found?"

"A second class of incision," Mei said. "Narrower than the exterior marks.
Adjacent to the warm lens. Not attributable at sight to known Argosy tooling."

There was a short silence at operations. Nadiya used silence well.

"Could the machine have made it on a later revisit?" she asked.

Idris answered before Mei did. "Not with the crawler in the marker room in its
present condition, and not with any tool geometry I recognise from the early
fleet specifications."

"Then I have enough," Nadiya said. "By local operations authority I am holding
all cutting and trenching east of grid E-54 pending scientific review. Mark the
site. Bring back images, thermal data, geometry, and the full node cache. Do not
sample the lens. Do not disturb the slab."

The decision arrived without theatrical weight. It was a line in an operations
log, and because it was a line in an operations log it mattered.

Mei acknowledged. Idris did the same.

The colony, on its eleventh sol of real occupation, had just decided that not
knowing was preferable to cutting first and explaining afterwards. Mei thought it
an excellent beginning for any civilisation and kept the thought to herself.

Idris fixed a temporary beacon at the slot mouth and linked it to Mule Three.
"If the conjunction relay had not deferred the original cache," he said, "this
would likely have gone to an Earth committee years ago."

"Would that have improved it?"

"It would at least have changed the paperwork."

They withdrew into the marker room. Mei made one final pass over the basalt slab
before leaving. The six incisions on its face were clear in profile. She had
already logged them as a count and not as language, which seemed to her the only
honest choice. Then, as she shifted her lamp lower to avoid reflection from the
glossy face, another line came up from the basalt beside the existing six.

She stopped at once.

The seventh incision was narrow and exact. It had been missed on the first pass,
or hidden by angle, or absent then. She could not yet rank those explanations.
She only knew that the count on her field record had become wrong.

"Idris."

He looked, checked the earlier images on his sleeve, and said nothing for a full
five seconds.

Mei recorded the new line, transmitted the correction to operations, and amended
the field manifest before memory could improve upon fact.

> OBJECT FACE INCISIONS: 6
> CORRECTION: OBJECT FACE INCISIONS: 7

The change occupied one character in the report and enlarged the site
considerably.
