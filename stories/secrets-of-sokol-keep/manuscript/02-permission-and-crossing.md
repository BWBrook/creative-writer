Liela's office had clean windows, swept boards, tied bundles of invoices, and a
map of Thorn Island pinned so squarely that Keria noticed it before the woman
behind the desk.

Liela followed her gaze. She was in her late thirties, narrow in the face, with
ink on one finger and no patience she wished to spend. "House Sokol does not
grant entry to armed strangers because tavern rumour has inconvenienced them."

Keria placed Markoth's account, Thirus's account, and Grim's name in order.
She left out the parts that made men sound foolish. Foolish men defended
themselves. Absent men had to be found.

Liela listened with her hands folded. "Igan Sokol is quartermaster at the keep.
He is young, but capable when properly directed."

"Who directed him?"

"House Sokol."

"And Sergeant Thornbuck?"

"The Black Fist assigns its own officers."

Aravele looked at the island map. "Then each office can blame the other."

Liela's eyes moved to her. "A crude way of saying it."

"A useful way."

The room held for a moment. Outside, cart wheels struck a rut. Somewhere on the
docks a man shouted about rope.

Liela took a sheet of paper and wrote a note in a hard, narrow hand. "Karst
will take you if I tell him to. You will speak to Darvag or Shandra on arrival.
You will make no claim on property. You will remove nothing without account.
You will report to this office."

"And if the Black Fist object?"

"They have an office three doors down. I recommend you let them object in
writing."

Hurn at the Black Fist post did not object in writing. He did not object in any
form that required him to stand. He was an old dwarf with a greying beard, a
stiff leg, and a desk that had defeated him years before. He read Liela's note,
snorted once, and searched a drawer for his seal.

"Grim Thornbuck," he said. "Lazy until treasure was mentioned. Then keen as a
tax collector."

"You knew about the treasure talk?"

"Everyone knew. Nobody believed it. Keep duty is cold stone, bad food, and rich
boys telling soldiers where mud belongs. Grim volunteered twice."

Keria took that in. "How many guards?"

"Six under Grim. If they are dead, I need names. If they are drunk, I need
names louder."

"And if they killed Igan Sokol?"

Hurn's hand stopped over the seal. It was the first stillness in him. "Then you
come back with proof. Bodies if you have to. Words if they will stand."

He pressed the seal into wax and gave her the paper.

Karst was waiting at the ferry steps as if someone had already told him how his
morning would be spoiled. His boat was broad, tarred, and scarred by years of
stone, salt, and bad landings. He read both notes, spat over the side, and waved
them aboard.

"Sit low," he said. "If you fall in, keep your mouth shut. The bay will provide
plenty without you helping."

The map made the crossing look like a finger's width. The boat disagreed. A
hard wind came sideways through the harbour mouth. The ferry rose, slapped down,
and shouldered through chop green as old glass. Keria held the gunwale and
watched Phlan pull back into smoke and roofs. Ahead on Thorn Island, the
lighthouse stood with its top unlit.

Karst rowed with his jaw set. "My grandmother said the island had priests
before Sokols, and priests before them, and things below the priests. She was
wrong about many things. She was wrong less often about water and the dead."

"You saw the servants yesterday," Aravele said.

"Darvag on the quay. Shandra at a window. Boy behind her. Scared, all three."

"Guards?"

"No."

"Igan?"

Karst shook his head. "Young Sokol used to come down and ask old questions.
How deep the bay was. Where the rocks sat at low tide. Whether lights looked
different in fog. Polite boy. Too polite for the stories he chased."

The ferry scraped the island quay. Darvag was already there, a small man in a
mended coat, with a mallet tucked into his belt as if repair work could defend
him. Behind him, uphill, Sokol Keep rose in old stone and newer domestic walls.
No banner moved above it. No patrol crossed the yard. The lighthouse tower stood
at the southern edge, its beacon chamber dark in morning cloud.

Darvag looked at the papers, then at Keria's face.

"You came for the light," he said.

"We came for the people missing around it."

Darvag read that better than either seal. He stepped aside and led them up from
the quay. The wind followed.
