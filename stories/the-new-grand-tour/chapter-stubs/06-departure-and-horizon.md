The final movement makes the story’s thesis explicit: O’Neill-1 is not a resort
bolted to a machine, but a functioning urban settlement that happens to receive
visitors. School timetables, council debates, rent complaints, Saturday markets,
delivery robots, doctors, marriages, and teenagers on benches all matter more
than the city’s symbolic glamour because they prove permanence from the inside.

The brief conversation with a resident planner sharpens that point. To residents,
“O’Neill cylinder” is investor language; “the station” or “home” is what they
actually say. That distinction is valuable continuity for any sequel centred on
people who live and work there rather than merely pass through.

The closing sections add two future-facing threads. First, rumours of larger
heliocentric industry and early Dyson-swarm-style infrastructure are already
circulating with practical seriousness. Second, the narrator boards Earth Direct
already thinking about returning and staying longer. Those two impulses — to go
back, and to go farther — are the natural launch points for follow-on stories in
this universe.
