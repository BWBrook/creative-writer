I am writing this in the departure lounge at O’Neill-1, in a chair that looks as though it was designed by a Scandinavian furniture house and approved by a life-support engineer. To my left, a school party from Santiago is eating sesame buns beneath a departures board that alternates between **Earth Direct**, **Shephard Packet**, and **Hub Recreation Excursion**. Ahead of me, through the shielded glass of the endcap gallery, Earth hangs in blackness with the Moon off to one side, the two worlds parted now by the clean geometry of the Earth–Moon L5 point. Three days ago I was in California, over-caffeinated and faintly doubtful. This morning I had breakfast in a rotating city.

The remarkable thing about cislunar tourism in 2052 is not that it exists. It is that it has become legible. One can understand it now in the old human terms: transit, hotel, district, view, meal, excursion, weather, price bracket. Not cheaply, not yet casually, but recognisably. A holiday beyond Earth no longer feels like a stunt performed by governments or billionaires. It feels, increasingly, like travel.

What follows is my account of the California–Moon–L5 circuit: the launch to orbit, the run out to Moonbase-3 (Shephard), two days under lunar gravity, the transfer onward to O’Neill-1, and a stay in two very different hotels on what is, by any sensible definition, humanity’s first true space city.

The journey starts at Pacific Meridian Terminal, just north of Vandenberg, where the old military severity of the coast has been softened by palms, brushed aluminium, and the sort of quietly expensive coffee that tells you civilisation always survives technological upheaval. The terminal is part clinic, part airport, part customs hall, and part theatre. Everyone tries to look relaxed. No one quite succeeds.

The clientele is broader than you might expect. Honeymooners. Engineers on combined work-and-leisure packages. A retired couple from Osaka who told me they had delayed Antarctica for twenty years and decided that if the world was changing this quickly they would skip straight to the Moon. There are children too, which still feels faintly indecent on a launch manifest, though statistically they are probably safer now than on the long-haul flights of the 2020s.

The old glamour of astronautics has been replaced by something at once more prosaic and more thrilling. You check in. Your luggage is weighed with pitiless accuracy. You are fitted, not for a pressure suit, but for a launch shell and a medical patch pack that keeps an eye on your hydration, vestibular response, and blood chemistry. One of the staff says, in exactly the tone once used for “window or aisle?”, “Do you prefer a stronger ascent sedative or the standard profile?” I choose standard and immediately regret my courage.

Our vehicle, the *Mojave Clipper*, sits out on the apron like a cathedral’s notion of a javelin. It is a fully reusable passenger stack, methane in the lower stage, aerospike transfer stage above, all of it turned round with the brisk efficiency that used to belong to ferries and budget airlines. The cabin is comfortable in the way high-end medical equipment is comfortable. There is no pretence that this is a cruise.

Launch itself is nine minutes of uncompromising persuasion. The engines light with a violence one feels less in the ears than in the bones, and California, the Pacific, and all the brown folds of the coastal ranges flatten into abstraction. Then staging. Then a brief, uncanny smoothness. Then orbital insertion and the first unmistakable sensation that up and down have become negotiable.

From there the choreography is almost disappointingly civilised. We dock at Concord Gateway, the orbital transfer ring, where most passengers spend four hours in a modest rotating hotel drum while their onward vehicles are fuelled and loaded. Concord is a reminder of how much the post-2035 automation surge changed the economics of near-Earth space. There are robot tenders everywhere, propellant depots tucked into the station’s shadow, and construction frames beyond the windows where autonomous welders move with insect patience. The age of one-off heroics ended when machine industry arrived in orbit and refused to leave.

My onward ship, the *Selene Packet*, is not luxurious, but it is humane. The passenger section sits on a short tethered ring that provides a gentle fractional gravity during cruise. There is a bar, a darkened observation lounge, and the kind of restrained cabin design that tells you every kilogram is still being argued over by accountants in three celestial locations. Yet even here the central miracle asserts itself: you leave California after breakfast, sleep in transit, and wake with the Moon filling half the forward display.
