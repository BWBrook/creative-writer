Moonbase-3, known almost universally as Shephard, lies near the south polar highlands. The tourist brochures, naturally, show the blue-white Earth hovering over silver regolith like a Christmas ornament. The truth is subtler and more beautiful. At the pole the light comes slantwise and seems never fully to decide whether it is morning or evening. The Earth sits low, nodding around the horizon over long periods rather than soaring overhead. Shadows are absolute. Sunlit ridges blaze.

We transfer from lunar orbit in a squat, reusable descent craft whose pilots are more supervisor than aviator, most of the work being done by a machine stack with vastly more patience than any human nervous system. The landing is clean, almost disappointingly so. No cinematic bounce, no rattling heroism. Just a precise settling into dust, a burst of procedural chatter, and the opening of the hatch into the filtered dryness of the base.

Shephard is not a resort pretending not to be infrastructure. That is one of its great strengths. It knows what it is. The main pressurised concourse is lined with basalt composite walls, embedded guide lights, public gardens of hardy greens, and transparent panels looking into machine rooms that appear to be in a permanent state of cheerful competence. The place hums. Pumps, heat exchangers, air handlers, water separators: the hidden organs of human life made semi-visible, which has the pleasing effect of making the Moon feel less like theatre and more like an inhabited frontier.

Tourist accommodation is in the Guest Ring, a clean and agreeable module with small rooms, excellent showers, and the best blinds I have ever encountered. Lunar sleep is peculiar. The body misses weight, the mind misses weather, and silence on the Moon is never true silence because the base itself is always doing the labour of keeping everyone alive. By the second night, that background hum begins to feel oddly protective.

Two days is exactly right for a first stay. The first day is for wonder. The second is for noticing.

Wonder is easily arranged. There is a guided rim walk at reduced pressure and, for the fit, a longer excursion by pressurised rover to a lookout above one of the permanently shadowed extraction zones. The old language of “mines” feels wrong here. What you see are clean-edged industrial apertures, freight crawlers, cryogenic handling plants, and rail lines disappearing into darkness. Lunar water is no longer a curiosity; it is the basis of the cislunar economy. Every hotel shower, every depot tank, every cup of coffee beyond Earth tastes, faintly and philosophically, of south polar ice.

There is leisure too. Shephard has a low-gravity sports hall where teenagers and overconfident journalists alike discover that bounding gracefully is harder than it appears. It has a small museum on early lunar settlement that manages to make events from the 2020s look both recent and antique. It has a greenhouse promenade where the air is warmer and wetter than elsewhere in the base and where, after an hour of moonlight austerity, the sight of basil and dwarf tomatoes can become absurdly moving.

What one notices on the second day is how quickly routine colonises the impossible. There are schoolchildren in uniform. Maintenance crews arguing amiably over a panel replacement. Shift workers carrying lunches. A barber. A dentist. A woman in a business jacket scolding a tablet while the Earth hangs behind her over the regolith. The Moon is still sublime. It is also, now, a place where people live.
