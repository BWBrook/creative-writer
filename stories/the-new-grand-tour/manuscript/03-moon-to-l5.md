Freight leaves Shephard by electromagnetic rail. If you are lucky, you will see the cargo sleds fired from the launcher trench: no flames, no romance, just elegant violence and a disappearing canister carrying water, metals, composites, or packaged volatiles into the economy that now loops between Earth orbit, the Moon, and the Lagrange points. Passengers, however, remain conservative and combustible. We leave by hopper.

The ascent from Shephard to orbit is brisk and businesslike, and from the depot above the pole we board the *Archimedes*, the L5 packet. This is where old science-fiction habits must be discarded. No ion engines patiently shoving a shiny shuttle across cislunar space. The packet uses a high-thrust transfer drive that burns long enough to let everyone feel a proper departure and then hands the voyage over to inertia and exquisite navigation. For the first twenty minutes we sit in couches while the ship presses us gently but unmistakably backwards. Then the engine cuts, the straps slacken, and cislunar space becomes once more a matter of motion too large to feel.

The run from the Moon to Earth–Moon L5 takes a little under thirty hours on the premium passenger schedule, which is fast enough to feel modern and slow enough to let the journey remain a journey. This, for me, is where the holiday becomes something rare and almost philosophical.

From the lunar surface and from L5 alike, Earth is still roughly a one-lunar-distance object. It does not loom larger as one approaches the settlement. Instead the geometry unfolds. At departure, Earth and Moon sit comparatively close in the same sweep of vision. As the packet arcs outward, the Moon falls away and the angle opens until the two worlds stand apart by something very close to sixty degrees, the clean triangular logic of the Lagrange configuration made visible to the naked eye. It is a beautiful corrective to terrestrial intuition. Space does not behave like a road map.

Passengers spend most of the voyage drifting, eating, reading, or pretending not to stare at one another’s faces as though to confirm that civilisation has become preposterously ambitious. I spoke with a young couple from Nairobi on their way to O’Neill-1 for what they called, with complete seriousness, “a city break”. That phrase alone would have justified the trip.

Late in the second day, the settlement resolves from astronomy into architecture.

O’Neill-1 does not gleam. This should be said at once. From a distance it looks less like a silver dream than like a heavily worked piece of industrial intention: a dark cylinder wrapped in shielding, banded with radiators, flanked by mirror assemblies that catch sunlight and feed it inward through light wells. Dock spines protrude from the hub. Service craft flicker around it. If you were expecting a crystal palace in vacuum, you will be disappointed. If you were hoping for something more convincing, you will be delighted.
