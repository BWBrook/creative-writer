Docking takes place at the hub, in microgravity, where hotels, freight handlers, customs kiosks, and free-floating luggage briefly mingle in a chaos that no station planner ever quite admits to in public renderings. The trick of O’Neill-1 is that it does not ask newcomers to pretend rotation is simple. It lets you negotiate with it.

![Arrival into O’Neill-1](assets/images/oneill-1-arrival.jpeg)

To reach the inhabited rim, one boards a spoke train whose cabin gradually acquires the station’s tangential speed as it travels outward. At first there is no weight. Then a hint. Then the soles of your shoes begin to matter. By the time the train reaches the outer districts, you are standing in something close to Earth normal, your inner ear mildly suspicious but no longer rebellious. At 500 metres radius, O’Neill-1 spins at a leisurely 1.34 revolutions per minute: slow enough that most people adapt by breakfast, though the station politely advises you not to whip your head about like an owl during your first evening.

Then the doors open, and one steps into the future people were always trying and failing to draw.

The interior of O’Neill-1 is eight kilometres long and half a kilometre in radius. That gives it about twenty-five square kilometres of habitable inner surface, which is enough for a city, not for a fantasy landscape. This matters, and it is the first thing I admired about the place. O’Neill-1 does not pretend to be a counterfeit Earth valley. It is emphatically itself: dense, vertical, cultivated, and proud.

The avenue outside Rim Station curves away and upward. Buildings rise five, ten, fifteen storeys, their bases planted on the inner hull and their tops leaning visually toward a sky that is in fact the rest of the city. Across the diameter, one kilometre overhead, the opposite side of the cylinder hangs above you: streets, trams, park belts, apartment towers, light wells, and moving windows, all upside down and yet quickly, disturbingly normal. You can watch a train cross the sky.

The “sun” arrives through long controlled light slots fed by exterior mirrors. Morning here is not a mechanical on-off, but a carefully tuned brightening. Afternoon has a proper golden slant. Evening is dimmed in stages, with spectral filters and the station’s thin managed atmosphere doing just enough to lend a hint of terrestrial dusk. It is an engineered day, but a graceful one.

Population at the time of my visit stood a little above forty-two thousand residents, plus a few thousand transients. That is enough to generate the indispensable urban luxuries of variety and anonymity. One can walk through O’Neill-1 without feeling that everyone already knows one’s room number and dietary preferences. That, more than any brochure promise, marks the threshold between outpost and city.
