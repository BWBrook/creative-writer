I spent my first two nights at the Spokehouse Transit Hotel, a three-year-old mid-market property in the Meridian district that has sensibly decided not to waste volume pretending to be palatial. It is the sort of place one would book on Earth for proximity and reliability, then remember fondly because it turned out to understand travellers better than more expensive establishments do.

Rooms are compact, but space architects have become virtuosos of fold-out dignity. The bed disappears into the wall by day. The desk emerges from it. Storage is built into every thickness. The shower is excellent. The lighting is warm rather than futuristic. My room faced inward over a tram line and a narrow linear park planted with silver-leafed ornamentals whose roots disappear into a station-wide greywater system. In the early morning I lay awake and watched commuters passing under a sky that contained another city.

The Spokehouse’s greatest asset is its honesty. Breakfast is not a performance. It is very good coffee, station-grown greens, cultured yoghurt, egg sheets from the protein co-op, bread baked in-house, and fruit from the orchard decks two districts over. One quickly learns that the food aboard O’Neill-1 is freshest where it is least apologetic. The city has excellent agriculture, but most of it is vertical, stacked, and tightly managed: hydroponic towers, mushroom vaults, algal bioreactors, dwarf citrus galleries, and grain culture in service rings. The best cuisine does not try to conceal these origins beneath nostalgia. It celebrates them.

At night the Spokehouse lobby fills with the social class every working city needs: technicians off shift, junior administrators, a surprising number of graduate students, tourists too curious to retreat to their rooms, and one or two residents who plainly enjoy watching newcomers stare upward at the opposite skyline. It is lively without being performative. If you want to understand what O’Neill-1 feels like when it is not selling itself, stay here first.

No traveller should leave O’Neill-1 having seen only their hotel district and a view gallery. The station repays exploration because it has already developed neighbourhoods.

Meridian, where the Spokehouse sits, is brisk, practical, and transit-rich. Foundry East, farther down-cylinder, is more industrial, with robotics plants, materials labs, and assembly floors visible behind acoustic glass. The Greenline districts are where the park belts and agricultural towers are interwoven most artfully: not countryside, exactly, but a carefully negotiated treaty between human nerves and urban density. There are shaded walks there, exercise tracks, reservoir gardens, and pollinator galleries humming behind mesh. If the future is kind, it will always contain places where someone has thought seriously about shade.

The guided infrastructure tour is worth every credit. It takes you, in a small group, through a service gallery above one of the primary farm stacks, where crops rise in luminous tiers and robotic pickers move with priestly discretion. You see water recovery membranes, atmospheric scrubbers, thermal loops, and the mirror control systems that choreograph daylight for the whole cylinder. None of it is glamorous in the old sense. All of it is exhilarating. The deepest pleasure of O’Neill-1 is that its civilisation is not metaphorical. It works. You can inspect the pumps.

There is recreation too, and some of it is unique to rotating habitats. Near the hub sits a low-gravity leisure sphere where residents and visitors drift through three-dimensional courts for a game halfway between handball and ballet. There are microgravity yoga classes, tethered swimming, and, inevitably, a restaurant that has made a minor cult of serving desserts that arrive as edible floating sculptures. I attended, in the interests of journalism, and can report that pistachio spheres behave better in 0.1 g than one might fear.

My favourite public space was Meridian Park, one of the station’s broadest green corridors. It runs for nearly a kilometre, which on O’Neill-1 counts as generous sprawl. There are trees there, though not towering ones: carefully selected and root-managed species that provide texture rather than forest. Children played under them while overhead, absurd and marvellous, the corresponding park on the far side of the cylinder hung upside down in the sky. It is impossible to become entirely used to that sight. One simply grows fond of one’s incomprehension.

For my final two nights I moved to the Aurelian Meridian, the station’s best-known luxury address and, like the Spokehouse, only three years old. Where the Spokehouse wins by intimacy and intelligence, the Aurelian wins by understanding that true luxury in space is not gilding but composure.

The property occupies a prime site near an endcap transit spur, with direct access to one of the shielded observation salons. Its public spaces are all curves, quiet stone composites, printed timber veneers, water sounds, and a confidence that says everyone involved knew how difficult this was and decided not to mention it to guests. The corridors still carry the faint scent of new construction: cedar, polymers, sealants, the smell of a civilisation freshly unpacked.

My suite looked inward across the city, but the room’s masterpiece was not the view from the bed. It was the timed access to the Celestium Lounge, an observation chamber embedded behind metres of shielding and smart shutters. Twice a day the shutters open in sequence for a limited viewing period, and guests gather, trying very hard not to resemble pilgrims.

It is, I am sorry to say, one of the finest hotel amenities on or off Earth.

From that lounge you see what no rendering captures properly: the black itself. Earth hangs there, luminous and inhabited-looking beyond language, while the Moon sits displaced from it by the precise geometry of L5. Cargo lights move in the distance. Mirror assemblies glitter far off the cylinder’s flank. Somewhere behind you, hidden in layers of metal and water and regolith shielding, a city of forty thousand has ordered another espresso.

The Aurelian’s restaurant is excellent in the way truly modern cuisine should be: local where possible, proudly synthetic where useful, and utterly uninterested in cosplaying the 2020s. I had charred farm-tower greens with lunar-brined cheese, cultured bluefin, fermented mushroom glaze, and a dessert whose principal components were citrus from the Greenline decks and protein silk spun into something between pastry and geometry. Had someone described this menu to an anxious columnist in 2025, they would have written an essay on decline. In 2052 it tastes like confidence.

If the Spokehouse shows you how O’Neill-1 lives, the Aurelian shows you what it is becoming. The clientele includes diplomats, founders, the better class of industrial magnate, and the sort of terrestrial old money that always eventually appears wherever novelty acquires chandeliers. Yet even here the city resists becoming pure spectacle. A family from Mumbai argued amiably over museum tickets in the lobby. Two habitat engineers, still in work boots, had drinks at the bar beneath an installation map of the L5 construction lattice. Luxury in space, for the moment, remains adjacent to labour.
