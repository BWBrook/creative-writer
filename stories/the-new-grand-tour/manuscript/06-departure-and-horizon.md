The temptation, when writing about places like O’Neill-1, is to lean too hard on the superlatives and neglect the sociological fact. So let me state the most important thing plainly: O’Neill-1 is not a resort bolted to a machine. It is a functioning urban settlement that happens to receive visitors.

This is why it is interesting, and why it is unexpectedly moving.

There are school timetables posted in the transit stations. There is a civic council whose debates I am told are already becoming satisfyingly petty. There are complaints about rent, district prestige, and noise from the Foundry line. There is a Saturday market where station artisans sell ceramics fired in low-pressure kilns and jewellery made from lunar alloys so discreetly that buyers can feel tasteful about it. There are doctors’ surgeries, civil marriages, teenagers sitting on benches pretending not to notice each other, and delivery robots whose route-optimisation algorithms have not prevented them from acquiring, in the public imagination, distinct personalities.

O’Neill-1 is the first place beyond Earth where one can feel the onset of the ordinary. I mean that as the highest praise. The ordinary is what permanence looks like from the inside.

Its very compactness contributes to this. Because the station is only 500 metres in radius and eight kilometres long, every district must justify itself. Land here is interior hull. Air is infrastructure. Water is treasury. You do not waste such things on decorative emptiness. And yet the settlement has found room for beauty: park belts, public art, small reservoirs, graceful trams, shaded concourses, views intentionally framed. It feels, oddly, more designed than many cities on Earth, but also less cynical. Life support discourages frivolity. It also punishes ugliness more quickly.

One afternoon I asked a resident planner, over coffee in Meridian Park, whether she minded living in a place often described by Earth media as “the first O’Neill cylinder”. She laughed. “Only by people on Earth,” she said. “We call it home, or the station, or L5. ‘O’Neill cylinder’ is what you say when you want investment.”

Every frontier city develops rumours before it develops legends. On O’Neill-1 the rumour most often repeated, and repeated with that half-dismissive excitement which is merely enthusiasm in disguise, concerns solar industry farther out.

Officially, the talk is of heliocentric power architectures, automated collector webs, and distributed materials processing. Unofficially, after a second drink, people use the older, grander phrase: Dyson Swarm.

No one serious imagines that next decade will see some complete shell around the Sun. The engineers here are not romantics in that sense. But there is widespread belief that the combination of lunar materials, cislunar robotics, AI-directed fabrication, and cheap orbital industry is about to move the centre of gravity outward again. First depots. Then stations. Then rotating settlements. Then, perhaps, the first true solar-scale industrial lattices: power collectors, smelter arrays, relay habitats, machine ecologies no longer tied emotionally to planetary surfaces.

From the Earth of thirty years ago, all this would sound like a fever or a cult. From the departure lounge at O’Neill-1, it sounds like Tuesday with funding.

This, finally, is what the California–Moon–L5 circuit gives you that no terrestrial holiday can. It does not merely offer spectacle. It offers perspective on sequence. You see the pieces fitting together. The California launch infrastructure that feels almost banal. The lunar ice economy that supports everything farther out. The rotating city at L5 proving that people will not merely visit space but inhabit it at urban scale. Once you have seen those three in one itinerary, the rumours of the next step do not sound absurd. They sound early.

My return ship boards in forty minutes. Around me the lounge has grown busier. The schoolchildren have gone. A woman in a business suit is dictating a contract amendment in flawless annoyance. Two tourists are comparing hotel notes and, I am pleased to report, arguing about whether the Spokehouse or the Aurelian better captured the spirit of the place. They are both right.

If you are deciding whether O’Neill-1 is worth the journey, the honest answer is that it depends what sort of traveller you are. If you want old Earth comforts re-skinned in vacuum, wait a few years. The market will oblige. If you want the pastoral fantasy of broad meadows under a curved sky, wait for a larger cylinder. They will come. If, however, you want to spend a holiday in a place that still feels fractionally ahead of itself, where engineering has only just relaxed enough to permit style, where the parks are real but the infrastructure is still visible, where one can drink excellent coffee beneath an upside-down tramline and then go upstairs to look at Earth from sixty degrees off the Moon, go now.

Go while the hotels still smell new. Go while the residents still roll their eyes at being called pioneers. Go while the phrase “city break at L5” still sounds faintly like a joke and has not yet hardened into routine brochure copy.

The great surprise of this trip has not been the Moon, though the Moon remains severe and magnificent. It has not even been the launch, which is still the most violent commute humanity has normalised. It has been the city. O’Neill-1 is compact, busy, self-aware, slightly smug, and entirely alive. It is not the future as children once drew it. It is better than that: the future as adults eventually built it.

Boarding has begun for Earth Direct. Beyond the glass, Earth shines with the old planetary authority that no city, however ambitious, can quite rival. Yet I notice, as I gather my bag, that I am not feeling the clean relief of return. I am already thinking, quite seriously, about coming back and staying longer.
