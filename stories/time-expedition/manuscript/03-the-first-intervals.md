Filby obtained his meeting on the Thursday after the Medical Man's fortnight
had expired. There had been letters, a visit to Richmond, and an afternoon
during which a Fellow of the Society examined the fish while the Traveller
tried to explain the machine. The Fellow asked to have the jar turned round.
Filby turned it. By the end of the visit the Fellow had undertaken to
communicate the account, though he declined to express an opinion upon it; and
the Traveller, who had prepared for a considerable argument, complained that
he had been given no opportunity to explain himself.

At Burlington House I found the Medical Man already seated near the front,
with an empty chair beside him. The Traveller was standing at a table, trying
to separate two sheets of paper. His hands were no longer bandaged, but he
could not make the finger and thumb of his right hand meet properly, and
presently he wetted a finger of the other. I had watched those hands moving
about the machine so often that their clumsiness distressed me. His coat
fitted him badly still. There was a glass of water within reach which he had
pushed behind the jar.

The room was by no means full. Some of the men had come to look at an animal;
others, I understood, to hear how its supposed age had been determined. Two
behind me were discussing a paper entirely unconnected with our business, and
continued until the chair was taken. Filby had the photographs in a stiff
cover upon his knees and was unwilling to let them out of his possession. The
whole machine remained at Richmond. On the table, beside the specimen, were
stoppered bottles, little sealed tubes, the discoloured notebook, and a
drawing large enough for the men at the back to see. It showed the ridge and
the waters beneath it. There was a very small square for the shelter.

One of the Vice-Presidents presided. He explained briefly how the
communication had come before them and asked that questions be reserved until
the account was finished. When he invited the Traveller to speak, the latter
looked down at his papers, moved them aside, and took up the notebook instead.

He gave the circumstances of his departure and the manner in which he had
fixed his first station. Most of this we had heard in the study. He pointed to
the places upon the drawing, and Filby passed the early photographs along the
front row. Once somebody asked which way the river ran; he answered, and
returned to the little square. The notebook lay open under his left hand.

"It was here," he said, "that I made the first advance. The early entries
describe what I found well enough. Thereafter you have mostly figures. I had
to get them while I could."

He looked up. I had brought paper to take down what he said, but I wrote very
little after that. The account which follows I completed with him at Richmond;
here and there, where he could recall nothing to add, the gaps remain. I
remember particularly that he spoke for a long time without once looking at
the drawing.

"I had been there about two days. The machine was under the rock, everything
that I meant to carry was secured upon it, and I had put the levers back. To
leave my stakes standing out in the sunlight while I went forward seemed, at
the last moment, an exceedingly foolish proceeding. I could picture a hundred
things happening to them. A beast would rub against one; the bank would slip;
somebody would pull it up. Somebody! I caught myself at that, and looked out
of the cleft as if I had heard a footstep.

"The plain was empty. I had seen no more of the large animal since it went
away from the river on the previous evening. A small insect was running up the
stone at the entrance, carrying something white, and it was the last thing I
watched before I moved the lever.

"I travelled slowly at first. There was no need for the headlong descent by
which I had reached that world, and I intended to stop after a year and a
little more. But I had never before travelled under so close a covering.
Beyond the mouth of the cleft the daylight fluttered, and the rock above me
remained black. I could not tell whether it was moving. I found myself
stooping lower and lower over the dials—though to stoop would have availed me
nothing if that mass had settled upon the place I occupied. I increased the
speed to get it over. For a little while a whitish illumination showed the
lower edge of the stone; then even that went, and I stopped at the first
return of light that I could distinguish, with the dials marking about
thirteen months.

"The roof was still above me. It was so low and so substantial that I put my
hand against it. I had been afraid of finding it upon my shoulders, and now
its mere resistance to my fingers reassured me absurdly. Dust had come in upon
the foot-rest. In getting down I struck my knee against a rail and swore
aloud; the sound went out of the shelter without an echo.

"I went to the entrance. The river had disappeared.

"At least, I could not see it. A pale brown obscurity filled the plain, dense
below and thinning upwards until the sun showed through it as a disc without
rays. The air had a curious dryness which caught at the back of my throat. I
stood blinking, with my hand still upon the rock. The ridge on which I had
built the cairn was visible, though its farther end seemed cut off; beyond
that there was nothing by which I could judge distance. A low bank of fog upon
the Thames would have hidden as much, but there was none of the freshness of a
fog. I had begun to sweat while I stood there.

"I waited a little, and made out one bush, then another. They were where I
remembered them. Close by, a lizard slipped out from between two stones and
lay in the sun. Its throat moved. That small and perfectly ordinary action did
more to bring me out of my bewilderment than the recognition of the ridge, and
I went down towards the river with quite a foolish eagerness to find my stake.

"It was there, leaning a little. The upper part had bleached. Below the notch
the wood was darker, and below that again a length of it stood clear which I
remembered driving through wet mud. I took it in my hand and shook it. It
held. The water was two feet three inches below my mark.

"I went down the bank to reach it, and in doing so broke through a thin crust
and sank above the ankle. There was soft mud beneath, warm and surprisingly
tenacious; my boot came out with a noise like a cork drawn from a bottle. I
laughed. There was something delicious in finding my own little piece of wood
weathered by a year—a year which had passed almost without touching me! Two
days ago I had cut that notch. This morning I could have picked rotten fibres
from beside it. I began to understand what I might do with the machine.

"It was nine by my watch before the haze lifted enough for me to see across
the river and distinguish the low bushes beyond it. The water moved quietly in
a narrower course, leaving a broad strip of its former bed exposed. There were
no fish in the little shallows; in the deeper reach below the ridge I saw them
moving, and once a small one broke the surface. I did not try to catch it. I
was looking at the stake again.

"There was no mark above the notch. I scraped the wood with my nail and
examined its whole length. Nothing had lodged against it. No tangle of
vegetation, no streak of mud, no line to show that the stream had risen in my
absence! You will remember the breadth of that channel on the drawing. It was
a bed made for floods; and I had left my witness standing in it through the
seasons of a whole year.

"I went up on the bank and opened the notebook. Before I had written a word I
was thinking of Filby, and of the gentlemen who could not tell him what had
become of their Permian animals. I wished one of them could have been there to
see the wood. I remember turning it a little, to bring the bare side into the
light, as though there were somebody at my elbow to whom I could point it out.

"That entry was written with great satisfaction. An unusually dry part of the
year had been the first explanation that occurred to me; but a year had
passed, and the wet part should have left its sign. I had found none, and I
wrote that there had been none. I was impatient to get to the lake.

"The way was more troublesome than before. What had been firm ground near its
margin was covered now with brittle scales of dried mud which slipped
underfoot and broke with a faint jingling sound. The sun was high enough to
make the shadow of my hat very small. When I came to the second stake, the
water lay sixty paces farther out.

"The new margin curved away from the old; it was not a simple shrinking of the
outline I had drawn. Here a tongue of mud projected, there a shallow pool
remained cut off. I could smell the warm water before I touched it. It left a
stronger taste upon my finger than the first sample had done, but I distrusted
that comparison, for my lips were dry and I had mud upon my hands. I filled
the bottle, stopped it, and sat down to write its label with the sun burning
the back of my neck.

"It occurred to me, as I sat there, that the river might have turned aside
somewhere above the ridge. There was so little fall across that plain! A bank
broken through, a new passage scoured a little deeper than the old, and the
water would be carried off in another direction, leaving my stake, and perhaps
the lake itself, beyond the reach of floods. I had not followed the stream to
its mouth, but I had supposed that it supplied the lake, and now their common
shrinkage seemed to confirm the connection. There was no need, on that
supposition, for the rains to have failed at all. Somewhere beyond my view
there might be green banks and a full river, while here the pools dried up and
the bare mud lay baking in the sun. Even the dust became intelligible. I began
to look with a considerable interest at the dry hollows between the bushes,
wondering which had once carried water, and how long it had taken the scrub to
establish itself in them. I meant to look for the new course from the cairn
that afternoon. For the present I had the bottle between my feet, still
without its label.

"The pencil point broke. I had sharpened it very carefully before leaving the
shelter, and the trivial mishap enraged me. I laid the bottle on its side
while I pared the wood, and for a minute or two forgot the lake, the Permian,
and everything but the necessity of getting a point upon that miserable
pencil.

"By the time I had finished the label I wanted to be back at the shelter.
Until then the work had pleased me. At Richmond it had seemed a matter of
taking a few readings at successive dates; I had made no allowance for the
walking between them, nor for doing again, with yesterday's stiffness still in
my back, everything I had done a year before. I wanted water from the stream,
and the stream was behind me across that glaring waste. My bottle held the
bitter water I had just collected. I took a mouthful from my drinking-flask
and found it warm.

"At two the thermometer stood at a hundred and four."

"In the sun?" said a man behind me.

"In the shade," said the Traveller. He looked towards the questioner for a
moment before he went on.

"I moved it farther from the sunlit rock, waited, and read it again. It
remained there. I had taken it out to the cairn with the camera, and left it
in the shadow while I repeated my three views. Even through the finder the
plain was altered. The distant ridge which had bounded the first view could
scarcely be distinguished; the white margin of the lake had widened, and a
bush close below me, by which I had fixed the direction, looked as though it
had been rolled in dust. I searched with the glass along the upper course of
the river, but lost it among the bushes. The haze hid everything beyond.

"The colour of the scrub troubled me. There was still much the same quantity
of it—so far as I could judge—and the little resinous trees and the fern-like
things stood separately over the red earth, with the bare spaces between them
as before; but the grey had spread over them, and when I rubbed a leaf between
finger and thumb, though I got dust upon the skin, the green beneath did not
look as I remembered it. I rubbed another, and then broke off a spray to take
back. I wished I had kept a leaf from my first visit. I put the spray beside
the thermometer.

"There were insects in the bushes still. Something bit me behind the ear while
I was occupied with the camera, and at the river I put up the same small
creatures from the mud. Once I saw a movement among the distant grazers,
though whether they were fewer than before I could not say. I mention these
things because I have been asked whether the country was already dead. It was
not dead. I was hot, thirsty, and very much alive myself, and I saw other
creatures going about their affairs with an indifference to my discoveries
which I found almost offensive.

"Towards evening the larger animal came down on the opposite bank. I saw it
first through a gap in the reeds. It stood for some time without moving, the
head turned a little towards me, and I had the glass upon it before it came
out upon the open ground. As it turned down the slope there was the pale mark
across the left shoulder. I recognised it at once. Then the angle changed and
I could no longer see the mark, but I continued to watch as though its
identity had been settled.

"It drank for a long time. Twice it raised its head and looked across at me. I
had been about to fill my second water-bottle, and I remained with the stopper
in my hand until I noticed that my thumb was trembling. There was no reason
why it should come across. It had seen me before and left me alone. I looked
along the water for the shallowest place.

"I thought of fetching the camera. The shelter was behind me, the animal was
in front, and the glass kept bringing those two teeth very close—yet when I
put it down I saw how far off the creature really was. I did not turn my back
to it. Presently it went up the bank and stood among the bushes, where I could
still see its head. I waited. An insect settled on my wrist, and I let it bite
before I moved. When I looked again the head was gone. I filled the bottle and
made for the shelter much faster than I had intended.

"The sample work took me into the dusk. I had brought the sealed tube of air
and the two water-bottles aboard, marked the readings, and compared them with
the first set; then everything must be secured for another start. My fingers
were stiff, and I undid the fastening twice before I got the last case aboard.
I left the smaller things where they lay and stretched myself upon the ground.

"I could not sleep. The cleft had been cool when I first chose it, but the
warmth that had entered during the day seemed to have got into the stone
itself. I turned my coat over to find a cooler part and found none. Through
the entrance I could see a little sky, brown at first, then dark; the stars
were faint, and one which I watched disappeared although no cloud was visible
about it. There was scarcely a movement of air through the crack at the back.

"I had intended to go forward after sleeping. Instead I lay listening. The
river made a sound now which I had not noticed at the first station, a small
intermittent trickling where the water passed over stones. At intervals some
creature called from the plain. I could not tell whether it was near the
entrance or a long way off. I got up once and looked out, and once I struck a
match merely to see the familiar metal beside me. The flame stood straight
until it burnt my finger.

"I considered waiting there for rain. A few days might have brought it; but
the food was packed for travelling, and I had already used more of it than I
cared to think about. I could go forward ten months in less time than it took
me to walk to the lake, and come once more near the season in which I had
first arrived, when there might be enough water to fill the old bed again,
even though the river had found another course. I lay trying to imagine it
washing about the stake, but I could see only the bare wood. I expected to
find the stream lower still.

"By morning I had slept a little, though I could not have said when. I drank,
ate without much inclination, and finished the packing. When I went to examine
the plumb, it hung above the chalk line, exactly as I had left it. I left it
hanging and made room for myself in the saddle. Outside, the brown obscurity
was beginning again to fill the lower ground. I took the levers in my hands
and started.
