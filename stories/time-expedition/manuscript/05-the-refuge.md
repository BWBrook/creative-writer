"The rain did not come that day. I sat until my legs were cramped, and then
went down to the stream with a bottle. There was still a little clear water
running over the stones. I lay upon my stomach to reach it, and the smell of
the bank came up into my face. I had to rest twice upon the way back. The
bottle, which I had carried empty without noticing it, seemed extraordinarily
heavy when it was full.

"For the remainder of that afternoon I did very little. I mended my coat. Both
shoulders had suffered in the first hauling of the machine, and the rents had
spread until the sun reached my skin through them. I sewed them with the twine
used for tying specimens, sitting with my back against the rock and the
revolver beside my knee. There were six cartridges in it and six in the case.
I counted them again when I had finished sewing, and buckled the case beneath
my coat. Then I put the instrument register inside with the notebook, and
watched the plain. I had grown tired of taking the same things out and
putting them back.

"The light failed very slowly. There was no sunset that I could distinguish,
only a reddening of the brown sky and a gradual loss of the farther ground.
Near the stream a small animal ran a little way and stopped. It ran again,
stopped again, and presently I could see it no longer. I wondered whether it
had reached the water. That occupied me for some time. I had made a theory
which required the destruction of a great many animals, but I found myself
hoping that this particular little beast would get a drink.

"Towards midnight there came a wind. It blew hot against the ridge and filled
the cleft with dust; my lamp went out, and I lay with my coat over my face,
listening to the loose things shifting upon the shelves. There was a sound
like rain which brought me up eagerly. It was the dry leaves blowing along
the stone. My hat went out with them. I saw it once against the lighter ground
beyond the entrance, and let it go.

"At daylight the wind had fallen. The sky was lower, with a yellowish light
under it, and I could see a darker bank hanging over the upper end of the
plain. I took two photographs from the cairn. The ground in the finder was
quite distinct, and the lake made a little white streak in one corner. It
seemed a poor representation of the place. One could not photograph the heat,
or the taste in one's mouth, or that incessant desire to lie down.

"I came back before noon and found the plumb still where I had left it. I put
my fingers into the crack beneath it. There was room for a nail at the widest
part, and a few small stones which had been tight in it were loose. I pulled
one out, examined it, and pushed it back. Then I boiled some water. My lips
were so sore that I could scarcely get the cup between them.

"The first drops fell while I was eating. They made black spots upon the
ledge, as large as pennies, with dry stone between. I put my hand out. A drop
struck it with an actual sting; then another struck the cup, and before I
could set the cup down the whole plain had vanished. The noise upon the block
above me was tremendous. I went out into it and stood with my mouth open.
Water ran down my neck and under my shirt, and I laughed. I remember laughing
quite loudly. It was as though I had been waiting for that rain all my life.

"For a time I thought the change would put an end to the whole business. Let
the river fill again, let the plants have water, and what became of my great
catastrophe? I should bring back a fish and an account of a dry summer. I was
very willing to bring back that. I set out every empty vessel I could find,
and watched them filling, and drank before the first was half full.

"But the water did not sink into the ground as I had expected. It ran over it,
gathering in little streams upon the upper shelves of the ridge, pouring from
one shelf to another, and bringing with it sand and twigs and fragments of
the dead scrub, until the clear rain that fell before the entrance was lost
in the red stuff below. Where a bush had stood there was presently a hole
with water turning in it. Once I saw a whole patch of earth begin to slide,
move a yard or so with the stumps upright upon it, and dissolve. I watched
that very closely. I had walked over the place the morning before, and
afterwards, when the rain thinned a little, I could see the bare rock shining
where my path had been.

"By evening there was water coming through the crack at the back of the
cleft. It ran down the chalk line and dripped from the wire. I shifted the
loose cases higher, and lifted the knapsack out of a puddle. There was still a
dry place between the machine and the entrance, and there I sat, with my knees
drawn up and my coat wrapped about me. I could no longer hear separate blows
of rain. Beyond that steady drumming was a deeper sound, which increased when
the rain slackened and which, at last, I recognised as the river.

"I slept there, wet through, and woke, and slept again. Flashes of lightning
showed me the entrance, and once I scrambled up under the impression that it
was morning. I was convinced, too, that I heard a man outside, calling the
figures of a sum. I listened, and heard the same figures repeated, and was
trying to answer him when I found that I had been speaking myself.

"The third morning was brighter. Rain still swept across the plain in long
oblique lines, but between the showers I could see the river filling its old
bed from side to side, with a roughness upon it like the roughness of a
crowd, and here and there something dark that checked in the current, turned,
and was hurried on. There was no mark left by which I could find the stake;
I looked for a bush that had grown a few yards above it, and that too had
gone. Presently one of the dark things turned quite over, lifting a great
tangled root into the air. It was an entire tree. Others came after it, far
larger than anything I had seen growing in all my walks, borne down from
somewhere beyond the country I knew.

"Towards noon I saw that the water was crossing the plain towards me. Above
the place where I had made my measurements there was a bend, and from its
nearer bank a broad red tongue extended into a hollow that had been dry. At
first it ended among the scrub. When I looked again it had passed the scrub,
and small waves were running down it. The stream was cutting itself a shorter
way along the foot of the ridge. I watched the head of it reach the rocks
below me, curl back, and begin tearing at the loose earth there. Presently a
bush dropped straight down without bending. It had lost the ground under its
roots.

"I took the camera out and made an exposure from the ledge. I wanted the old
channel and the new water in one view, and had to lean out to get them. As I
did so I saw three of the heavy, low-built animals climbing towards me in
single file, the first stopping to nose at the rock and the others crowding
against it, while the last, which was scarcely clear of the water, kept
slipping and recovering itself. They found a way up at the farther end of the
ledge and passed above the cleft, so near that their feet showered grit upon
my shoulders. One looked down at me. Its wet face had a stupid, troubled
appearance, and I was glad when it went on.

"There was no reason to remain. I had seen the change I came to see, and the
food was nearly finished. I put the camera upon its shelf and began packing.
It was a wretched business. The plant papers had swollen so that I could not
close the press, and when I knelt upon it to force it together the string
slipped and a sheaf of the wet leaves spread across the mud. I tried to
gather them in their order. Everything I touched left red mud upon something
else, and I had to stop and shake my hands to loosen the fingers. The
satchel upon the machine was wet outside, but its fastenings held, and I
pushed at the jar and the little tin to make certain that they could not move.
I ought to have been satisfied with those. I went back for the press.

"A stone fell into the water at the entrance. I did not see where it came
from. I heard the splash and looked out, still holding the string. The ledge
had lost a piece from its outer edge, and the red water was running against
the foot of the cleft. Upon the left, where the great roof-block rested,
there had been a slope of packed earth and broken stone. Little heaps of this
were dropping away. The harder rock above stood out farther with each fall.

"I put down the press and tried the machine. It was steady, and the saddle
was clear. I had kept a narrow passage beside it, on the right as one looked
into the cleft. On the left were the shelves and cases; behind it the two
feet of space I have mentioned. I meant to haul it back along the ridge to the
ground upon which I had first arrived, and went out to see whether the ledge
would still carry it. The string was entangled about my fingers.

"I was upon the right of the entrance, with my hand against the wall, when
the animal came up. Its forefeet appeared first over the broken edge; then
the long head, swinging a little as it sought a hold. It scrambled upon the
ledge and stood between me and the open plain. Mud ran off its belly. I could
see the ridge of the old scar upon its left shoulder, pale against the wet
skin, and the ribs moving behind it. The head turned towards me.

"I had got the revolver out. I do not remember drawing it. The beast's mouth
was open, and its lower jaw moved sideways a little, as though something had
stuck between its teeth. I shouted at it. It checked, crouched lower, and
came at me.

"My first shot struck the stone beside its foot. I saw the splinter fly. The
next caught it high upon the foreleg, or so it seemed from the red streak
which appeared there. It turned with a harsh, coughing cry and plunged into
the entrance. I had stepped sideways along the ledge, and stood with the
revolver out, waiting for it to come back.

"The smoke blew away. The beast was in the cleft.

"It stood across the narrow space in front of the machine, with its head
towards me and its tail against the cases. Behind its neck I could see the
bright rod upon which the whole action of the machine depended. I shifted a
little, trying to bring the rock behind it instead. The edge crumbled under
my heel. There were four charges still in the revolver; I could have fired
every one of them into that place. I kept seeing the rod break.

"For a little while neither of us moved. Water ran over my boots and out
between two stones. The animal breathed with its mouth open. From somewhere
under the block came a grinding sound, almost hidden by the river, and I saw
the plumb move. It drew slowly away from its mark, stopped, and drew away
again. There was no swinging. The peg from which it hung was moving with the
stone.

"I called out, quite foolishly, and the animal thrust its head forward. Then
the left-hand shelf came down. It fell in a heap of bottles and thin slabs,
and the beast scrambled over it into the space behind the machine. There it
was pressed crosswise against the back wall, with its head beside the saddle.
A corner of the roof had settled upon the fallen stuff; the entrance was
lower, and through a gap upon the left water burst in with a rush, carrying
sand across the floor.

"I got into the passage on the right. I had to go upon my knees. The revolver
was in my way, and I thrust it into the case beneath my coat and began pulling
at the stones. A flat piece had fallen across the foot-rest, with one end
under a case. I could not lift it. I dragged the case towards me instead.
Something glass broke inside it; then its lid came off in my hands, and the
water took little packets of paper out of it and turned them over and over
about my knees. I pushed the empty box into the stream. The slab was loose
now, and I heaved it against the opposite side of the cleft.

"The animal was moving. I heard its feet scrape, and saw its head pass behind
the farther rail. It had turned to come out on the left, where the shelves
had been. There was not room for it. It pushed, drew back, and pushed again;
the frame rocked against my arm. I caught hold of the saddle and pulled the
machine towards me. It scarcely stirred. A heavy piece of stone had lodged
against the lower rail, forcing it inwards, and held that side down.

"I tried to pull the stone away. It was rounded, slippery with mud, and each
time I got my fingers beneath it the thing settled upon them. I drove my
shoulder against the frame and tried again. The pain in my right hand was so
sharp that for a moment I could see nothing. Then the stone rolled, the rail
sprang a little, and I fell backwards into the water. I had freed it. I lay
there stupidly, looking at my hand.

"There was another movement overhead. A thin edge of stone slid down the
wall and struck me above the left ear. It made very little impression upon
me at the time; I remember putting up my hand because something warm ran
inside my collar. I crawled back to the saddle. The camera hung by its strap
from a broken end of the shelf, swinging just beyond my reach. I stretched
out for it. In that instant the animal got its head past the rail and opened
its mouth within a yard of my face. I saw the two long teeth, the smaller
teeth between them, and a thread of water hanging from its jaw. I drew my
hand back and caught the frame with it.

"I got one knee upon the foot-rest. The machine leaned as I put my weight
upon it, and I passed my left arm between two bars to keep myself from
falling. I could not sit upright. The roof touched my back, and I lay forward
over the dials, fumbling for the levers. I found them together in the pocket,
but could not separate them with my right hand. I had to free the other arm,
fit them one at a time, and thrust the arm through again. All this took a
long time. I heard the animal struggling, and the water, and a continual
little cracking close above my head.

"The starting lever moved. The roar broke into separate blows, the entrance
flickered, and the animal became a brown indistinctness beyond the bars. Then
it was gone. The roof seemed to press closer; I bent my head until my face
was almost upon the glass. I dared not stop. Presently there was light upon
the dials, and I raised my head a little and found the stone no longer above
me.

"I went on. There were black intervals in which I saw nothing beyond the
machine, and intervals of wavering light. Once I thought there was water
about me. I watched the hands and kept the motion up. What happened to the
cleft, whether the whole block fell or only that corner, and whether the
animal got out, I cannot tell you. I have thought a good deal about the
animal. At the time I wanted only to leave enough years behind me to be sure
that it could not come any farther.

"But there was one more thing to do before I could come home. I had dragged
the machine forty yards from the place at which I first arrived. It would
bring me back forty yards from my laboratory. When at last I approached our
own century I slackened, looking for open ground. I saw a wall take shape,
and the back of the house beyond it, and stopped in sunlight among long wet
grass. It was years before I had bought the place. The laboratory had not
been built.

"I knew the wall and the angle of the house. Between them I could find the
ground upon which the laboratory was to stand. I had to get the machine
there. It would not slide evenly with the bent rail, and I moved it by
lifting one corner and then another, a few inches at a time. Twice I lay down
upon the grass. There was a bee going in and out of a flower close to my
face, and from the house came the faint sound of somebody playing a piano.
I listened for a little while, trying to recognise the tune. Then I got up
and went on with the hauling.

"Before I started again I tore strips from my shirt and bound my hands. I
could not tie the knots with my fingers, and had to draw them tight with my
teeth. The mud upon the rails was drying. I wiped the dial glasses with the
inside of my sleeve, got into the saddle, and hooked my arm through the frame
again. The house altered, the laboratory came about me, and I slowed until I
could follow the smallest hand. I meant to keep my appointment. But the
stopping movement hurt my hand abominably, and when at last I made it the
machine struck the boards upon one corner and I heard them split. There were
men coming through the doorway. I looked past them at the clock."
