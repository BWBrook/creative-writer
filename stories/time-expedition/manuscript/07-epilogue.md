We went down to Richmond on the Saturday after the meeting. The Time Traveller
had asked no one else, and after dinner we sat over our cigars and a bowl of
smoking bishop in the little room. He had drawn the table close to his chair,
for he still used his right hand awkwardly; but he looked more like his old
self than I had yet seen him. Filby, who had been unusually considerate during
dinner, began presently to argue with him about the fire. The Traveller wanted
another lump of coal upon it. Filby said the room was already insufferable,
and put it on.

For a time we talked of common things. I remember telling him of a man we had
both known, who had gone to live in Italy; and his surprise at this quite
ordinary piece of news reminded me how much there was still to tell him. He
asked two or three questions about the man's affairs. Then he fell silent,
holding his glass against his cheek, and I thought that he was tired.

"Well," said Filby, "you have had your meeting."

"I have."

"Was it what you expected?"

The Traveller put down his glass. "I thought there would be more difficulty
about believing me."

"There was enough."

"At the beginning. Afterwards they took the journey very easily. It was the
explanation they would not have. I had spent a good deal of time preparing to
prove that I had been somewhere."

He looked across at me and smiled.

"The Geologist," he said to Filby, "is a very obstinate man."

"He was quite civil."

"Oh, perfectly! But I had not expected a man to be pleased to hear that I
had travelled two hundred and fifty million years, and then tell me it made
no difference."

Filby laughed at that, and the Traveller laughed too, though less readily.
Then he began to explain something about the course of the river. He spoke
with his eyes upon the fire, drawing the bend of it with one finger upon his
knee; but before he had finished I saw him shake his head.

"No," he said. "He would have answered that."

"What would he have answered?"

"That it was still the same river. I cannot get out of that valley, Filby.
Each time I try it, I find myself describing another part of the place."

"You got out of it very well at the end."

"Very badly. And I had meant to come away with so much! I thought the
photograph of the plain was going to spare me half a day's measuring. You
remember it? There is no telling whether the distance is a mile or ten."

I said that we could at least distinguish the shape of the country.

"Yes; but not what was growing upon it. Not enough to compare. I ought to
have walked nearer while I had the strength for it. There was plenty of
daylight. But I was in a great hurry to see what came next."

He took up his cigar again. There was an edge of vexation in his voice which
I had not heard when they were questioning him. I had been thinking of the
things lost in the flood; it had not occurred to me that he might be more
troubled by an opportunity which had passed while he was at ease. Filby watched
him for a little while before he spoke.

"Would another photograph have satisfied your Geologist?"

"No."

"Nor a dozen?"

"Of that place? No. He might admit that the trouble extended as far as they
showed. There would still be all the rest of the earth. And he might be right
about that, too. It might have gone on very much as usual beyond those hills.
I do not believe it did; but I never crossed them."

He said this without looking at either of us, and for a little time we let
him smoke. I had looked forward to this evening, when the account should be
finished and we could talk of it freely; yet I found myself wishing to change
the subject. The fire had burnt through the fresh coal, and the little jet of
gas which came from it made a thin singing noise.

"I am glad you are here, at any rate," I said.

He looked at me quickly. "So am I. You need not suppose I enjoyed all of it.
I wanted to see this room again most abominably."

"And now you have seen it," said Filby, "will you stay in it?"

"For how long?"

"Don't begin in that way," said Filby. "You know what I mean."

The Traveller moved his right hand upon the arm of the chair and looked down
at it. The finger and thumb were still stiff. He tried them together, slowly,
and left off.

"I shall not be fit to do much for some time."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have at present. What would you have me say? That I
shall never go near the machine again? I should not believe myself."

"I should like you to consider what happens here when you go near it."

"I do consider it. If you suppose—"

"You told us to wait until half past twelve."

"And until dinner. I came back before dinner."

Filby made an impatient movement. "You were carried up to bed before dinner.
There is a difference."

The Traveller said nothing to that. He sat with his head bent a little and
his cigar between the fingers of his left hand. Filby had leaned forward to
speak, and remained so, looking across the table at him; then he sank back
again. I heard a door shut somewhere in the house, and Mrs. Watchett's steps
passing along the hall. We listened until they had ceased.

"I have been a great nuisance to her," the Traveller said.

"She does not complain," said Filby.

"No. She would be easier to deal with if she did."

He reached for the bowl and filled our glasses. I thought at first that he
meant to evade the matter altogether; but presently, when Filby was occupied
with his cigar, he spoke again.

"I was afraid of the return long before the water came in. I could always
put my hand upon the lever; but I had done that once before, and it had
brought me into a room with three years' dust upon the furniture."

"You did not come home because you were afraid of coming home?" said Filby.

"No. I stayed because I thought I knew what was happening. I am telling you
what I was afraid of. There were times when I lay in that hole and tried to
reckon what I should do if I found the house shut up again. I used to begin
with Mrs. Watchett, and wonder whom I should find in her place. And then I
should find myself going through the same calculation upon the dials, though
there was nothing new to put into it."

I asked him whether he still thought the early experiments had caused the
obstruction.

"I cannot account for it in any other way. That is very little reason for
you to accept it."

"And you have not been trying it again?" said Filby.

"No. You know what the machine was like when I brought it back."

He glanced towards the laboratory door. There was nothing in the movement
to distinguish it from a hundred such glances I had seen him give; yet I
followed it, and for an instant the closed door seemed a very slight thing
to have between us and that colourless interval he had described. Beyond it
were the bench, the tools, the four dials; things which I could have touched
and named. I knew that the difficulty was not upon the other side of a door.
But I was glad that he had not left it open.

"You ought to have told the Astronomer," I said.

"I mean to, when he comes down. I should like him to see the machine first.
He may ask me something I have not thought of."

"And if he cannot explain it?" said Filby.

"He will have had a very reasonable excuse for coming to Richmond. I have
been to Burlington House with less."

He smiled, and then grew serious again.

"I do want to know," he said. "You must allow me that."

Filby turned his glass about upon the table. At last he said that he had
never objected to the Traveller's wanting to know anything; and the Traveller
did not press him to say more. We sat for some time longer. There were things
in my notes of the meeting which I wished to ask about, but I put them off,
and presently we began to speak of other matters.

Before we left I went into the laboratory. I had a wish to see the machine
again, after our talk of it, and Filby came with me. The Traveller brought
the lamp. He held it well away from the frame while Filby examined the bent
rail, which had not been straightened; and in that light the whole machine
looked more damaged than I remembered it. I asked whether the men from the
Society would find it in working order.

"They can begin with it as it stands," he said.

Filby had bent down to look past the saddle. Now he straightened himself,
and pointed.

There was a narrow strip of white paper curved against the rim of one of the
dials. It had been ruled in pencil with divisions of unequal length, and at
one end these had been rubbed out and drawn again more closely. A little of
the paper projected beyond the rim.

"What is that for?" said Filby.

The Traveller set down the lamp and stooped over the dial. I could not see
his face.

"It is not right yet," he said.

Filby looked at me. Then he picked up the lamp, and held it lower.
