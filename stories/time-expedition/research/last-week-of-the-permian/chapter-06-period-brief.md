# Chapter VI Period and Continuity Brief

This brief records the bounded checks made while drafting Chapter VI. The
canonical research dossier remains authoritative; the choices below are open
to Astra's critique.

## Meeting procedure

The setting is the special meeting already fixed by Chapter III: Burlington
House, a Thursday afternoon, a Vice-President in the chair, questions reserved
until the account is finished. The Society does not vote on a communication;
the chair's close therefore "receives" the account, refers the specimen and
samples to Fellows for report, and proposes a small deputation to Richmond to
see the machine, which the dossier permits. No public demonstration occurs.
Questioners keep occupational identities. The communicating Fellow of Chapter
III, who examined the fish at Richmond and declined an opinion, is identified
here as the Comparative Anatomist.

## Geology

- Borings under London (Meux's brewery, Tottenham Court Road, 1878; Ware,
  1877) passed from the Chalk and Gault directly into Palaeozoic rocks with no
  Permian or Trias between. The Geologist uses this to say that no exposed or
  bored bed under London can confirm the Traveller's district, and that any
  Permian beds once there may have been removed before the Chalk. This
  matches `world.md`: no terminal-Permian deposits survive at modern Richmond.
- Red beds occur in the Devonian (Old Red), Carboniferous, Permian and Trias
  (New Red); the Geologist's point that colour is not a date is period
  commonplace (Ramsay 1878).
- The Magnesian Limestone of Durham, source of *Palaeoniscus* and *Acrolepis*,
  is a marine deposit; the Traveller's fish came from a stream. The Geologist
  and Anatomist both use this.
- Prolonged Australian droughts and stock losses were current news in the
  late 1890s (the drought beginning 1895). The Geologist's New South Wales
  comparison is within a London Fellow's knowledge.
- The Geologist's pleasure at a large figure reflects the real late-century
  dispute in which geologists sought far more time than physicists allowed.

## Astronomy and physics

- Dial arithmetic: 92 thousand million days is about 252 million years; the
  Astronomer rounds to two hundred and fifty millions.
- Kelvin's limits on the age of the sun and earth (1862; restated 1897 at
  twenty to forty million years) make the dial figure unacceptable to the
  physicists and welcome to the geologists. The Astronomer states the quarrel
  without resolving it.
- Orbital speed of the earth about 18.5 miles per second; rotational speed
  at London's latitude some hundreds of miles per hour; the solar apex in
  Hercules (Herschel). All are period figures.
- The Galilean ship analogy (a stone dropped from a masthead) is the
  Traveller's own attempt at the anchoring problem; he states its failure.
  No mechanism is supplied. Latitude (high noon sun, short shadows) is noted
  by both men and left as "either Richmond has moved, or the pole has", which
  keeps polar-wandering speculation period-bounded and unasserted.

## Chemistry

- A few cubic centimetres of air permit a rough oxygen determination by
  absorption but not a carbonic-acid figure to the parts in ten thousand that
  would matter (about three in ten thousand in 1890s air). The Chemist says
  so and predicts he will find air.
- Sulphuretted hydrogen, iron sulphide and decaying vegetation account for
  the third-station water and black deposit; lead-acetate paper darkens in
  any such pond, as the Traveller said in IV.
- The cool year after Krakatoa (1884) was attributed to the dust veil. Dust
  cools by intercepting sunlight; Tyndall (1859–61) showed aqueous vapour and
  carbonic acid absorb the earth's radiant heat; Arrhenius (1896) computed
  warming from a doubling of carbonic acid. The Chemist uses these to remove
  the Traveller's dust mechanism and substitute a gas, without endorsing the
  Swedish computation. This is the only place modern backstage (volcanic
  carbon) enters, and it enters as a Victorian conjecture from an examiner.

## Comparative anatomy

- Rhomboidal enamelled scales in oblique rows, heterocercal tail, small
  numerous teeth: a palaeoniscid-grade ganoid. The Anatomist compares fin
  position with *Acrolepis* and refuses species; he allows "Permian or
  Carboniferous", the two systems sharing such fishes.
- Owen's Theriodontia (1876) rests on Cape jaws and teeth. The Anatomist
  reduces "theriodont" to resemblance, asks whether the animal lapped (a
  tongue would bear on the mammal-like question), and notes the Traveller's
  four-toed print with a fifth toe set back is not a reptile's foot as then
  understood.
- Identity of the scarred animal across three sightings is granted as
  probability, not certainty, per the design.

## Continuity

- Filby's testimony matches Chapter I (departure at eleven) and II (return
  at four; bent rail; red mud; revolver fired twice, now in his bench
  drawer). The Medical Man's testimony matches II (burns as from Egypt; fever
  from heat, thirst and sleeping wet).
- The narrator's published obituary is from Chapter I.
- Filby's red-marl joke (Chapter I) is echoed by the Geologist without his
  knowing it; the narrator notes the coincidence.
- The Traveller's "how I know where I was" (Chapter II) is the Geologist's
  first question; "some of you mean to take the mountain" (Chapter IV) is
  answered by the Geologist.
- Custody at the close: the fish goes to the Anatomist with its label; tubes,
  bottles and packet go to the Chemist against a receipt; the notebook stays
  with the Traveller. The epilogue's altered settings and the temporal
  obstruction are not touched.
