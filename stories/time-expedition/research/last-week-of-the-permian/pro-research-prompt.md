## Paste-Ready Prompt

You are the research collaborator for a historical science-fiction project.
Conduct rigorous, source-backed research and produce a practical research
dossier for *The Last Week of the Permian*, a proposed further adventure of
H. G. Wells's Time Traveller.

This is research for fiction, but it is not a request to draft fiction. The
governing conceit is that the story was written by Wells in the late 1890s,
left unpublished, and recovered much later. It must therefore feel like a
genuine product of Wells's intellectual and historical moment, including the
possibility of intelligent period-authentic mistakes. Modern science is a
backstage aid to coherence, not knowledge automatically available to the
Traveller or Wells.

Read the attached approved story design in full before researching. Treat its
five-act architecture, narrative roles, temporal rules, dramatic movement,
and ending as fixed. Do not reopen or replace them. Use the attached text of
*The Time Machine* as the sole story-world canon. Do not import details from
films, sequels, adaptations, fan works, or later Time Traveller stories.

The research priority order is:

1. Wellsian authorship authenticity.
2. Late-1890s epistemic authenticity.
3. Internal scientific coherence.
4. Modern scientific plausibility.

This order matters. A deliberate late-Victorian interpretation that modern
science regards as incomplete or wrong may be preferable to a perfectly
modern explanation placed anachronistically in the Traveller's mouth. Modern
accuracy should expose accidental incoherence and make divergences deliberate;
it should not turn the story into modern hard science fiction in Victorian
costume.

### Fixed Story Context

The Traveller departs from Richmond, Surrey, and travels roughly 251.9 million
years into the past, near the Permian-Triassic transition. His machine remains
attached to the terrestrial frame and to the crustal tract that will become
Richmond. It follows that tract through continental drift but cannot travel
laterally. The physical mechanism remains unexplained.

He cannot target a particular day or extinction pulse at such temporal depth.
After arriving, however, he can make shorter traverses through time at the
same locality to compare successive conditions. These traverses are tiring,
imperfectly calibrated, and dangerous because each stop assumes that the local
space remains occupiable.

Ancient Richmond should offer a varied but defensible local environment if the
evidence permits it: some combination of elevated rock, river plain, and a
nearby shallow coast, inland sea, lake, or substantial watercourse. The
Traveller drags the machine into a nearby cave or cave-like refuge and uses it
as shelter, store, laboratory, and temporal observatory.

During a calmer phase he deliberately collects and preserves one complete
small animal. A larger terrestrial predator initially ignores him because he
is neither recognised prey nor a meaningful threat. As environmental collapse
produces hunger, displacement, and desperation, its behaviour changes. The
climax combines the predator's intrusion or approach with geologically and
environmentally motivated failure of the refuge. The Traveller escapes with
his notebook, a compact sample set, and the complete small specimen, while
abandoning much of his evidence.

He later presents the expedition to a Royal Society audience. Serious
questions come principally from a Geologist, an Astronomer, a Comparative
Anatomist, and a Chemist or natural philosopher. The audience provisionally
accepts the journey and physical evidence while remaining divided over the
Traveller's dating, causal interpretation, and extrapolation from one locality
to a planetary extinction.

The title *The Last Week of the Permian* describes the local sequence the
Traveller experiences and reconstructs. It must not imply that modern science
has established a literally synchronous seven-day global extinction.

### Research Objective

Build a dual-layer dossier with a story-facing synthesis:

1. **Modern reconstruction:** the best current evidence for the setting,
   organisms, environmental changes, extinction mechanisms, chronology, and
   observable effects.
2. **Late-1890s knowledge:** what a well-read scientific investigator and
   relevant Royal Society specialists could plausibly know, call, measure,
   infer, dispute, or misunderstand between approximately 1895 and 1899.
3. **Wellsian translation:** how modern underlying events could become direct
   observations, period terminology, plausible inference, and disciplined
   uncertainty in the fictional account.

Do not collapse these layers. For every major recommendation, distinguish:

- what is physically happening under the preferred modern reconstruction;
- what the Traveller can directly observe or measure;
- what late-1890s words and analogies he could use;
- what explanation he could plausibly advance;
- how a relevant Society specialist could challenge it;
- where the manuscript may intentionally diverge from modern understanding.

### Questions To Answer

#### 1. Place, palaeogeography, and local geology

- Under one or more current plate-reconstruction models, where was the crustal
  tract now underlying Richmond near the Permian-Triassic boundary?
- What can be said responsibly about latitude, continental position, climate
  belt, elevation, drainage, and proximity to coast, inland sea, lake, or major
  river? State uncertainties and model dependence. Do not imply kilometre-scale
  precision that deep-time reconstruction cannot support.
- Is the desired mixed landscape defensible? If not exactly, what nearby or
  functionally equivalent landscape is the smallest adjustment?
- What rock or landform could plausibly supply a cave, overhang, lava cavity,
  fissure, abandoned channel, or other refuge? Distinguish a true karst cave
  from looser cave-like possibilities.
- Which modes of refuge failure could follow from the selected catastrophe:
  tremor, subsidence, roof fall, sediment movement, flood, thermal cracking,
  gas accumulation, or another mechanism? Recommend one primary option and at
  most one reserve option.

#### 2. Timing and causal sequence

- Summarise current dating of the end-Permian crisis and distinguish the
  Permian-Triassic boundary, extinction pulses, background deterioration, and
  recovery.
- Explain what is established, debated, or weakly supported about Siberian
  Traps volcanism and proposed kill mechanisms, including warming, carbon-cycle
  disturbance, ocean anoxia or euxinia, acidification, ozone damage, wildfire,
  aridity, toxic emissions, metal loading, or other relevant processes.
- Assess which effects could plausibly produce acute local changes over the
  Traveller's sampled intervals. Do not stack every proposed mechanism into a
  disaster catalogue.
- Propose one restrained, cumulative sequence of six to ten observable stages.
  It should begin with individually explicable irregularities and end in local
  ecological and physical crisis. Temporal traverses may sample a longer real
  interval while the Traveller experiences roughly a week of personal time.
- Explain what the evidence would and would not allow him to infer about
  distant volcanism and global extinction from one locality.

#### 3. Local ecology and organisms

- Reconstruct a plausible terrestrial and freshwater, coastal, or lacustrine
  community for the selected palaeogeographic setting. Avoid treating famous
  South African or Russian taxa as cosmopolitan without evidence.
- Identify plants, invertebrates, aquatic organisms, herbivores, scavengers,
  and predators useful for baseline observation and progressive ecological
  disruption.
- Recommend two or three candidates for the complete small zoological specimen
  and two candidates for the large predator. For each, assess geographic and
  temporal plausibility, likely ecology and behaviour, fossil evidence,
  recognisability to a modern reader, and suitability for the story.
- For the preferred specimen and predator, identify the names, classifications,
  reconstructions, or closest fossil analogues available by 1899. If the modern
  taxon or name did not yet exist, explain what the Traveller and Comparative
  Anatomist might responsibly call it instead.
- Separate direct fossil evidence from behavioural inference. The predator's
  change must remain ordinary animal behaviour under extraordinary pressure,
  not monster-villain behaviour.

#### 4. Observable and measurable evidence

- Identify signs available to unaided senses: sky, light, temperature, wind,
  water, odour, deposits, vegetation, animal movement, carcasses, tremors,
  shoreline change, and sound.
- Identify measurements feasible with compact late-Victorian field equipment.
  Consider thermometry, barometry, humidity, compass work, simple microscopy,
  water or gas sampling, chemical reagents, notebooks, chronometers, balances,
  collecting equipment, and photographic apparatus only where supportable.
- Distinguish effects that would be observable from effects known only through
  modern proxies. Do not let the Traveller perceive isotopes, global models,
  invisible gases, or microscopic mechanisms without an appropriate period
  observation or test.
- Recommend a compact expedition kit consistent with 1890s availability, the
  machine's limited carrying capacity, and Wells's practical imagination.
- Recommend plausible containers and preservation methods for air, water,
  mineral, botanical, and zoological samples. Establish whether spirits,
  formalin, drying, pinning, arsenical preparations, or other methods were
  available and appropriate at the proposed date.

#### 5. Late-Victorian scientific knowledge and nomenclature

- Establish what educated British geologists and naturalists around 1895-1899
  understood by "Permian," its stratigraphic position, its fossil assemblages,
  and the apparent termination or replacement of forms at its close.
- Determine whether a Royal Society lecture on the unexplained termination of
  Permian life would be historically plausible, and what narrower question or
  period phrasing would make it credible.
- Find period terminology for extinction, faunal succession, catastrophism,
  uniformitarianism, volcanic action, climate change, noxious gases, deep time,
  continental geography, fossil reptiles, and relevant physiological stress.
- Track terminology carefully. For each important word or taxon, state whether
  it was attested by 1899, what it meant then, and whether its modern meaning
  differs. Never back-project a later name merely because it is familiar now.
- Use Wells's attached *Text-Book of Biology* as evidence of his scientific
  idiom and conceptual repertoire, while checking palaeontological and
  geological claims against appropriate contemporary sources.

#### 6. Royal Society setting and examination

- Identify a credible late-1890s venue, meeting form, audience, chairing
  practice, demonstration convention, and route by which the Traveller might
  give an hour-long address associated with the Royal Society. Distinguish an
  ordinary scientific meeting, invited discourse, informal gathering, and any
  institution that is often confused with the Royal Society.
- Explain how specimens, notebooks, instruments, witnesses, and perhaps the
  machine could be presented or inspected without turning the scene into a
  modern conference or courtroom.
- For the Geologist, Astronomer, Comparative Anatomist, and Chemist or natural
  philosopher, propose three or four substantial questions each. Every
  question should use period-credible priorities and vocabulary, expose a real
  evidentiary limit, and elicit information useful to the reader.
- Include the Astronomer's spatial-anchoring question: why does the Traveller
  remain with Earth rather than materialising elsewhere as Earth and the Solar
  System move? The Traveller may report behaviour and speculate about
  terrestrial matter or gravitation, but the research must not pretend the
  mechanism is solved.
- Use occupational identities rather than proposing historical celebrities as
  characters.

#### 7. Canonical machine constraints

- Check the attached *The Time Machine* only for relevant evidence about
  movement, stopping, material intersection, visibility, rematerialisation,
  physical carrying capacity, fatigue, and the machine's relationship to its
  location.
- Report source passages or chapter references that constrain the proposed
  short traverses and cave station. Do not conduct a new prose-style study and
  do not infer rules from adaptations.
- Identify any unresolved mechanical question that should remain deliberately
  vague rather than receive an invented technical answer.

### Evidence Standards

For modern science, prioritise peer-reviewed primary research, authoritative
review articles, major scholarly syntheses, geological surveys, museum or
university collections, and recognised palaeontological databases. Prefer
recent work where it materially changes interpretation, but include older
foundational research when necessary.

For the historical layer, prioritise sources published no later than 1899:
original papers, monographs, textbooks, proceedings, catalogues, dictionaries,
museum records, and Royal Society or other institutional archives. Use modern
histories of science to locate and interpret period evidence, but cite the
period source whenever it is accessible.

Treat modern popular articles, general websites, and Wikipedia as orientation
only, not final evidence for a substantive claim. Do not cite search-result
snippets. Do not fabricate bibliographic details, quotations, page numbers,
DOIs, dates, taxonomic histories, or URLs. When a source cannot be accessed or
a claim cannot be verified, say so directly.

Label important conclusions with one of these evidence states:

- **Established:** supported by strong convergent evidence.
- **Supported:** a good working choice with meaningful but non-fatal limits.
- **Contested:** credible competing interpretations remain.
- **Speculative:** useful as a possibility but unsafe to present as settled.
- **Unsuitable:** conflicts materially with evidence or period authenticity.

Distinguish observation from inference. Distinguish a fossil's known anatomy
and occurrence from reconstructed behaviour. Distinguish absence of evidence
from evidence of absence. Where palaeogeographic or taxonomic uncertainty is
large, preserve it rather than manufacture precision.

Every material factual claim should carry a nearby citation. Use Markdown
links or numbered footnotes that survive export to Markdown. Include DOI or
stable archive links where available. Use short quotations only when exact
period wording matters; otherwise paraphrase.

### Required Report Structure

Return one self-contained Markdown document of roughly 7,000 to 10,000 words.
Shorter is acceptable if the evidence does not justify the target. Do not add
length merely for completeness. Begin with this YAML front matter:

```yaml
---
title: "The Last Week of the Permian: Dual-Layer Research Dossier"
project: "Time Expedition"
document_type: "external_research_input"
research_date: "YYYY-MM-DD"
knowledge_cutoff: "State the effective search date"
status: "for_project_review"
---
```

Use these sections:

1. **Executive findings**: no more than fifteen high-value conclusions, each
   tagged with an evidence state.
2. **Method and limitations**: databases and source types searched, date
   limits, major access gaps, reconstruction assumptions, and nomenclature
   method.
3. **Recommended local reconstruction**: a concise preferred setting, with at
   most one alternative and a clear explanation of uncertainties.
4. **Modern scientific reconstruction**: place, chronology, mechanisms,
   environment, organisms, and event sequence.
5. **Late-1890s knowledge and language**: what was known, disputed, measurable,
   and nameable, with a modern-to-period terminology table.
6. **Story-facing translation matrix**: for each major phenomenon, list modern
   process, direct observation, available instrument or sample, period term,
   Traveller's plausible inference, specialist challenge, evidence state, and
   intended modern divergence if any.
7. **Organism shortlist**: tables for community background, specimen
   candidates, and predator candidates, followed by one recommendation for
   each dramatic role.
8. **Refuge and climax options**: recommend one geologically motivated refuge
   and failure sequence, with one fallback only.
9. **Victorian expedition kit and exhibits**: compact equipment, preservation
   methods, sample sequence, what survives, and what is credibly lost.
10. **Royal Society examination**: setting recommendation and useful questions
    by occupational role.
11. **Canonical machine constraints**: only findings relevant to this story,
    with references to the attached source text.
12. **Anachronism watchlist**: terms, concepts, instruments, taxonomic names,
    behaviours, social practices, and scientific assumptions to avoid or
    qualify.
13. **Intentional-divergence register**: candidate places where a coherent
    Wellsian conclusion may differ from current science. Mark recommendations
    as provisional rather than making creative decisions on the author's
    behalf.
14. **Open decisions**: no more than ten genuinely consequential questions
    requiring project judgement. Do not include matters the approved story
    design has already settled.
15. **Source ledger and bibliography**: separate modern primary/review sources,
    pre-1900 primary sources, and later historical syntheses. For each central
    source, state briefly what claim it supports and any important limitation.

Tables must remain readable in plain Markdown. If a table cell would require a
paragraph, use a compact entry and explain it below the table.

### Required Discipline And Stopping Rule

Do not draft scenes, dialogue, descriptive passages, chapter outlines, or
Wells pastiche. Do not assess sentence-level style. Do not rewrite the approved
five-act architecture. Do not design the anthology spine. Do not solve the
temporal anomaly. Do not recommend famous historical scientists as characters.
Do not turn every possible end-Permian hypothesis into an event in the story.

Prefer a small number of defensible recommendations over a catalogue of every
possibility. Research to the point at which the project can make responsible
choices about setting, organisms, observations, equipment, nomenclature, the
refuge climax, and the Royal Society examination. Then stop. Clearly identify
residual uncertainty rather than attempting to eliminate it through repeated
speculation.

The report is an external research input. The project team will independently
review its claims, inspect decisive sources, and decide what enters the
canonical story dossier.
