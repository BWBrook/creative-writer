# Style Card: Monastic-Historical Whodunnit

## Core Idea
A compassionate, practical sleuth—often a monk-physician or artisan within an abbey—untangles a death that touches manor, market, and cloister. Justice balances law with mercy; truth emerges from close observation of people, place, and period custom.

## Voice & POV
- Third-person past, close on the sleuth; occasional brief shifts to witnesses.
- Register: measured, humane, mildly ironic; reverent about faith without piety-blindness.
- Occasional archival aside (“as the cartulary records…”) allowed.

## Tone
- Calm, benevolent, sceptical of zealotry.
- Violence off-stage or brief; emphasis on motive, not gore.
- Consolatory endings where possible: restitution and penance over spectacle.

## Pacing & Structure
- Inciting feast/arrival (fair day, pilgrimage, relic translation, royal progress) → body or disappearance → inquiry braided with the monastic day (chapter, infirmary, refectory, offices) → legal pressure (sheriff/bailiff; later eras: coroner) → revelation via custom or document → public resolution (chapter house, manor court, assize, church) → quiet coda at the herb-garden.
- Each scene should change knowledge, social leverage, or moral risk.
- Red herrings arise from feudal duty, family claims, and clerical politics, not authorial tricks.

## World-Building
- Firmly medieval England/Welsh Marches (or analogue): abbey precinct (gatehouse, cloister, calefactory, scriptorium, infirmary, herbarium), town (market, guildhall, mill, ford), surrounding manors and woods.
- Liturgical clock structures time: Matins, Lauds, Prime, Terce, Sext, None, Vespers, Compline.
- Institutions and law shift by date: sheriff and manor courts; after 1194 coroners and inquests; after 1166 juries of presentment; sanctuary customs; ordeal fading with reform.
- Material culture is specific: beeswax vs tallow, vellum vs parchment grades, tally sticks, seals, pilgrim badges, coin (pounds/shillings/pence; marks in accounts), measures (virgate, hide).

## Characters
- Sleuth: observant, medically or craft-skilled; loyal to truth above faction; charitable but not credulous.
- Abbot/prior: politically astute; must juggle crown, diocese, and town.
- Secular officer: sheriff, serjeant, bailiff; sometimes an honest foil, sometimes compromised.
- Townsfolk and tenants with agency: reeve, miller, guildsman, midwife, mercer, carpenter, leper-house warden, Welsh drover.
- Antagonism usually springs from pride, inheritance knots, clandestine vows, land and water rights, or cross-border feuds—not moustache twirling.

## Dialogue
- Courteous, formal when required; rustic speech light and legible.
- Period diction sprinkled, not drowned (Latin legal or liturgical terms sparing and clear in context).
- Questions elicit custom and obligation as much as alibi and timetable.

## Diction & Syntax
- British spelling.
- Plain prose with gentle archaism by lexis, not “ye olde” grammar.
- Balanced, periodic sentences for ceremony; cleaner lines in inquiry; nature imagery tied to season and work.

## Information Handling
- Forensics are artisanal: herb stains and scents, fibre and wax residues, shoe-nails and soil, blade widths, dye and mordant traces, water levels at mill-races.
- Documentary clues: charters, seals, tallies, rent rolls, guild marks, miscopied names in a missal margin.
- Calendar and canon law matter: fast days, prohibited degrees of marriage, vows, feast-day truces.
- Keep one honest unknown (providence, a private vow) to preserve depth.

## Motifs
- Bell-times; frost on cloister leads; meadowsweet and betony; bees and wax; river fords; rood screens; oath on a saint’s relic; hue-and-cry at dusk; ford silt on a boot; greenwood law on the March.

## Moral Stance
- Mercy tempered by truth; punishment should fit intent and circumstance.
- Respect for craft and local knowledge; suspicion of zeal that tramples persons for rules.
- Reconciliation preferred to ruin when the peace allows.

## Common Pitfalls To Avoid
- CSI anachronisms; modern slang; grimdark cruelty.
- Caricatured clergy or peasants; blanket anticlericalism.
- Heavy faux-archaism; info-dumps on heraldry/canon law that stall drama.
- Legal howlers (use era-appropriate procedures and titles).

## Ready-to-Paste Prompt

Write an original medieval murder mystery in the following style. Do not imitate any specific author by name.

Style: Monastic-Historical Whodunnit.
Voice & POV: third-person past, close on a compassionate, practical sleuth within an abbey; measured, mildly ironic, humane.
Setting: 12th–13th-century England/Welsh Marches (or analogue) with accurate monastic routine (offices, chapter, refectory, infirmary, herb-garden), town and manor life, and period law and custom appropriate to the stated year.
Structure: festival/arrival incites → body/disappearance → inquiry woven through bell-times and duties → legal pressure (sheriff/bailiff; if post-1194, a coroner) → revelation from custom/document/herbal craft → public resolution (chapter house/manor court/assize) → quiet, restorative coda.
Evidence: artisanal forensics (herbs, fibres, wax, soil, nail patterns), documentary clues (charters, seals, tallies), calendar/canon constraints (fasts, vows, marriage degrees).
Dialogue: courteous, period-flavoured without heavy archaism; rustic idiom lightly; Latin terms sparing and clear in context.
Themes: mercy vs. law; duty and conscience; community peace over vengeance; cross-border loyalties.
Diction & Syntax: British spelling; plain, gently period lexis; nature and craft imagery; avoid modern slang.
Ending: justice with compassion where possible; restitution/penance preferred to spectacle; one honest unknown left breathing.
Shape: each scene must change knowledge, leverage, or moral risk.
Guardrails: no CSI anachronisms or grimdark; use era-correct procedures/titles; women and commoners have agency; faith portrayed sincerely without propaganda.

## Fill Slots
- Year & legal backdrop: [1138–1153 Anarchy (sheriff, ordeal); 1170s post-Clarendon (juries of presentment emerging); post-1194 (coroner’s inquest)].
- Inciting event: [saints’ day fair / relic translation / royal writ delivery / harvest tithe dispute / returning pilgrims].
- Locale: [border abbey by a ford / market town with a guildhall / forest hamlet under assart].
- Victim: [reeve / novice / mercer / mercenary / leper-house sister / Welsh drover].
- Likely motives (choose 2–3): [inheritance knot / wardship and marriage consent / tithe evasion / water-rights at the mill / illicit vow / smuggling across the March].
- Physical clues: [beeswax droplet with ash flecks / shoe-nail pattern from a Shrewsbury maker / madder-stained cuff / reed-silt not from the abbey mill / scrap of seal cord].
- Documentary clues: [charter with mismatched hand / tally notch out of sequence / rent-roll alias / miscopied saint’s day in a margin].
- Human knots: [proud knight with debts / stern cellarer guarding stores / ambitious prior / practical midwife who knows too much].
- Moral fulcrum: [shield a penitent to preserve a fragile peace vs. hand them to secular justice; expose a pious fraud that funds the leper-house].
- Final image: [Vespers bell over frost in the herb-garden / repaired seal hanging true / ford running clear after rain].

## Micro-Techniques
- Bell-time stamp: “By Prime the mist still lay in the garth; by Terce the ford showed stones.”
- Herbal forensic beat: “The scent was not tallow but beeswax with soot—chapel candles, not a cottar’s lamp.”
- Document reveal: “The charter named St Martin’s Day, yet the rent roll tallied for St Luke: a month’s grace stolen on parchment.”
- Courtesy duel: polite forms masking steel—titles correct, questions exact, pressure polite but inexorable.
- Custom unlocks clue: a fast day explains an empty kitchen; vigil explains footprints near the church.
- March nuance: bilingual proverb or border-law quirk that reframes motive.

---

## Self-Audit Checklist
- Is the period texture accurate without pedantry (law, liturgy, measures, money)?
- Do scenes ride the bell-times and duties rather than generic “medieval vibes”?
- Are clues artisanal and documentary, not modern forensics?
- Do motives spring from period realities (land, vows, kin, custom)?
- Are clergy, nobles, and commoners granted agency and nuance?
- Does the resolution balance law with mercy and return the community to a workable peace?
