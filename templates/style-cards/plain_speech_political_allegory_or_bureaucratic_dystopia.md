# Style Card: Plain-Speech Political Allegory & Bureaucratic Dystopia

## Core Idea
Clarity as a weapon. A modest person (or an allegorical community) collides with a system that corrupts language to control reality. Hope begins as common decency; power answers with euphemism, scarcity, surveillance, and ritualised hate.

## Voice & POV
- Fable mode: cool third-person chronicler; impartial, unsentimental, ironical.
- Dystopia mode: close third or first past; observational, precise, self-policing thoughts.
- Register: plain, concrete, Anglo-Saxon lexis; rhetoric only for slogans and decrees.

## Tone
- Austere, lucid, morally indignant but never shrill.
- Compassion for ordinary folk; contempt for cant.
- Ironic calm in the face of cruelty.

## Pacing & Structure
- Fable arc: revolt → brief equity → managerial caste consolidates → rules revised → terror justified as necessity → tragic recognition.
- Dystopia arc: small heresy → furtive love/friendship → glimpse of a freer past → arrest and logical torture → capitulation and memory-rewrite.
- Scene fuel: queues, meetings, chants, ration books, minutes of denunciation, paper-shredders, printing forms, correction slips.

## World-Building
- Institutions with blunt names (Ministry, Board, Committee); slogans in capital initials.
- Shabbiness: broken lifts, weak tea, stale bread, peeling posters, defective bulbs.
- Language engineered: restricted dictionaries, shifting commandments, numbers that never add but always “improve”.

## Characters
- Protagonist: ordinary, decent, mildly sceptical; clings to unapproved memories or small fairness.
- Confidant: lover/friend who shares food, jokes, or a song; precarious refuge.
- Apparatus: smiling instructor, clever interrogator, efficient clerk, heroic propagandist; each believes in the machine.
- Crowd: fearful joiners; a few brave fools; one informer you misjudge.

## Dialogue
- Plain, short, transactional.
- Slogans and set phrases recur; officials speak in policy tautologies.
- Pub/workshop talk is wary; truth is told sideways.

## Diction & Syntax
- British spelling.
- Short declaratives; occasional long periodic sentence for panoramic indictment.
- Repetition for emphasis; triads; antonymic reversals.
- Prefer concrete nouns (bread, boots, ink) and active verbs (print, ration, erase).

## Information Handling
- Facts appear as notices, ledgers, wall charts, doctored photographs, redacted minutes.
- “Correction” scenes show how yesterday’s truth is deleted; absence looks tidy.
- State metaphysics explained by showing its effect on appetite, sleep, love, and memory—not by lectures.
- Leave one deep unknown: how far the machine truly reaches.

## Motifs
- Posters that watch; nursery rhymes turned into doctrine; commandments repainted at night; clocks striking the wrong hour; a paperweight; a forbidden tune; animals who copy their masters; a dictionary that shrinks.

## Moral Stance
- Freedom begins with calling things by their names.
- Power degrades language to make cruelty seem arithmetic.
- Small loyalties are dangerous and necessary; betrayal is a system outcome, not only a sin.

## Common Pitfalls To Avoid
- Cyber-gadgetry; neon grit; baroque world-lore.
- Villains who monologue; heroes who sermonise.
- Purple prose; nihilistic endings without moral clarity.

## Ready-to-Paste Prompt

Write an original political allegory/dystopia in the following style. Do not imitate any specific author by name.

Style: Plain-Speech Political Allegory & Bureaucratic Dystopia.
Mode: [Fable / Dystopia].
Voice & POV: [cool third-person chronicler / close third or first past]; plain diction; irony through fact, not flourish.
Setting: a shabby, centrally managed society with bluntly named institutions (Ministry, Committee, Board). Resources scarce; slogans everywhere.
Plot shape:
– Fable: revolt → brief fairness → managerial caste consolidates → rules revised → terror as necessity → tragic recognition.
– Dystopia: small heresy → furtive friendship/love → forbidden memory → arrest and logical torture → capitulation → tidy revision.
Language as control: show shrinking vocabulary, shifting rules, doctored records, ritual denunciations. Use slogans and triads sparingly but memorably.
Scenes: queues, meetings, poster campaigns, canteen meals, clandestine talk, document correction, interrogation.
Diction & Syntax: British spelling; short declaratives; one periodic sentence per section for panoramic indictment; concrete nouns; active verbs.
Themes: truth vs. euphemism; loyalty under pressure; how scarcity and fear enforce lies.
Ending: morally legible; either tragic recognition or narrow, honest survival. Leave one deep unknown.
Shape: each scene must change knowledge, risk, or complicity.
Guardrails: no tech porn; no villain lectures; no purple prose; avoid caricatured classes—complicity is ordinary.

## Fill Slots
- Slogans (triad or antinomy): [“Plenty Is Thrift.” / “Peace Is Production.” / “Clean Hands, Clean Thought.”]
- Institution names: [Ministry of Measure / Committee for Correct Speech / Board of Fair Shares].
- Forbidden act: [keeping a diary / teaching an old song / hoarding tools / unauthorised repair].
- Propaganda artefact: [poster with moving eyes / handbook of Correct Words (edition numbers matter) / repainted commandments over the barn/ward notice].
- Corrective mechanism: [public confession meeting / ration downgrade / remand with “instruction” sessions / photo retouch office].
- Human hinge: [betrayal by a well-meaning colleague / child informant / lover flipping under pressure].
- Final image: [fresh paint drying over a revised rule / a paperweight set back on a cleared desk / a song hummed too softly to hear].

---

## Micro-Techniques
- Slogan triad: “Work Is Freedom. Freedom Is Order. Order Is Peace.”
- Correction beat: “By noon the notice had no misprint; by dusk it had no past.”
- Plain indictment sentence: a long, calm catalogue of small shortages that add to tyranny.
- Antonym turn: “They called it a Victory Week; they cut the bread.”
- Object correlative: bread ration, dull razor, cracked mug, paperweight, children’s chant.
- Witness aside: one flat line from a clerk that reveals complicity: “It balances.”

---

## Self-Audit Checklist
- Is the prose plain enough to sting?
- Do power and language intertwine on the page (not just asserted)?
- Are institutions concrete and shabby rather than baroque?
- Does each scene change complicity, risk, or knowledge?
- Are slogans disciplined and memorable, not parodic?
- Is the ending morally clear while leaving one honest unknown?
