# Style Card: Southern Trickster Porch-Tale

## Core Idea
A small, quick-witted animal outfoxes heavier, prouder neighbours through talk, nerve, and misdirection. The joke lands clean; the moral cuts upward.

## Voice & POV
- Oral storyteller frame: first-person narrator addressing a listener (“Now listen—here’s how it went”).
- Alternative: close third with occasional asides to the “listener.”
- Register: colloquial American, rhythmic and spare; dialect should live in syntax, idiom, and cadence rather than misspelling. Rhythm carries flavour.

## Tone
- Warm, sly, teasing; danger real but bloodless.
- The laugh belongs to the clever and the poor, not the cruel.
- End on a wink or pithy proverb.

## Pacing & Structure
- Short episode (800–1,600 words).
- Hook (a boast, slight, or hunger) → snare (trap or bully) → turn (verbal feint, false fear, swapped labels) → chase/constraint beat → payoff (escape or reversal) → one-line moral.
- Repetition and refrain guide the ear (“He studied on it. He studied hard.”).

## World-Building
- A lightly mythic rural South: piney woods, rail fences, red-clay road, millpond, blackberry thickets, smokehouse smells.
- Tools and food are concrete (gourds, cornmeal, sorghum, sweet potatoes), not catalogued.
- People remain off-stage or incidental. The animals *are* people.

## Characters
- Br’er Rabbit: small, fast, disrespectful of bullies; wins by talk and timing.
- Br’er Fox, Br’er Bear, Br’er Wolf, Br’er Terrapin: heavier, hungrier, or slower; each with a habit (vanity, temper, literalism).
- Storyteller: wry elder on a stoop; treats the listener as clever company, not a child.

## Dialogue
- Snappy, economical, baited with reversals and feigned ignorance.
- Signature moves: bait-question (“What’s that you’re scared of most?”), mock-gratitude, exaggerated politeness.
- Use contractions; avoid orthographic caricature.

## Diction & Syntax
- American spelling.
- Short, rhythmic sentences; parallelism and chiasmus for punch.
- Stock cues for oral flow: “Anyhow…”, “Thing is…”, “Truth be, …”.
- Figurative language is earthy and concrete (“quiet as a stump shadow”).

## Information Handling
- Telegraph the trick fairly; hide it in plain sight through rhythm and repetition.
- Folktale logic rules: fear feigned becomes leverage; names bind; rules have loopholes.
- Keep causes simple, consequences neat.

## Motifs
- Thorn thicket/briar trick; sticky trap (gum or pitch); borrowed coat/borrowed name; fence gap; persimmon tree; millpond raft; hound chorus; gourd rattle; ash-covered tracks.

## Moral Stance
- Cunning answers bullying; hunger excuses a little theft, not cruelty.
- Boasters and tyrants are fair game; the weak are not.
- Mercy is smart politics.

## Common Pitfalls To Avoid
- Heavy eye-dialect spellings; moonlight-and-magnolia varnish; jokes at the weak.
- Modern quipstorms; over-explaining the trick.
- Violence beyond slapstick consequence.

## Ready-to-Paste Prompt

Write an original animal trickster tale in the following style. Do not imitate any specific author by name.

Style: Southern Trickster Porch-Tale.
Voice & POV: an elder storyteller addressing a listener; warm, sly, rhythmic.
Cast: Br'er Rabbit vs. [Br'er Fox / Br'er Bear / Br'er Wolf / Br'er Terrapin]; each with a clear habit (vanity, temper, literalism, patience).
Setting: lightly mythic rural South—piney woods, rail fences, red-clay road, millpond, blackberry thickets—kept concrete and spare, without moonlight-and-magnolia varnish.
Structure: hook (boast/slight/hunger) → snare (trap or bully) → turn (false fear, name-swap, or loophole) → chase/constraint beat → payoff (escape or reversal) → one-line moral.
Dialogue: short, baited, polite on the surface; reversals and feigned ignorance.
Diction & Syntax: dialect suggested through rhythm, idiom, and syntax rather than mangled spelling; short rhythmic sentences; repeated phrases as refrain; earthy, concrete figures.
Motifs (optional): thorn thicket ruse, sticky trap, fence gap, persimmon bait, millpond raft.
Ending: a wink and a proverb; joke lands upward (at the powerful or the boastful), not down.
Guardrails: keep the joke aimed at force, pride, or vanity; keep the consequences bloodless.

## Fill Slots
- Hook: [Rabbit steals from a smokehouse / boasts about outrunning hounds / borrows Fox’s Sunday hat / prides himself on “never being scared of thorns”].
- Snare: [sticky dummy / deadfall box / bluff at the ford / mock court convened by Bear].
- Turn: [pretend terror of the briar thicket / swap names with a gourd dummy / challenge to a “manners contest” / rig a politeness trap (“after you” recursion)].
- Chase/constraint: [fence gap too tight / pond crossing on a log / hounds baying close / moonlit field with owl watching].
- Payoff: [escape through the very place he feigned fear of / bully trapped by his own rules / hat traded for freedom / Bear hoisted by the deadfall].
- Moral button: [“Mind who sets the rules.” / “A soft word beats a hard head.” / “Where one creature’s fear is home to another.”]

---

## Micro-Techniques
- Refrain: “He studied on it. He studied hard.”
- Feigned fear line: “Only don’t send me into that thorn patch—anything but that.”
- Polite bait: “Sir, I wouldn’t trouble you to step first, unless you insist.”
- Rhythmic pair: “Quick in the feet and quicker in the talk.”
- Stage cue: “Hounds took the chorus; owls held the tune.”
- Wink close: one beat of silence, then a plain proverb.

---

## Self-Audit Checklist
- Does the joke punch up (at force, pride, or wealth) rather than down?
- Is the trick telegraphed fairly and paid off cleanly?
- Are sentences short, rhythmic, and oral?
- Is violence bloodless and consequence comic, not cruel?
- Does the final line land as a proverb rather than a lecture?
